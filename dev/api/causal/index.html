Spatial Causal Inference

Treatment effects when one unit's treatment reaches its neighbours: exposure mapping, difference-in-differences decomposed into a direct and a spillover effect, synthetic control with a spillover-screened donor pool, and shift-share designs, where the dependence runs through shared industry exposure rather than through geography.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.ExposureMap — Type
ExposureMap

A treatment vector together with its spatial exposure columns, returned by exposure and ring_exposure.

An exposure mapping is the object that makes a spillover estimable. SUTVA assumes unit i's outcome responds only to D_i. An exposure mapping replaces that with a named, finite summary of the treatment around i. The spillover parameter is then defined relative to that summary, rather than left implicit.

Fields

  • D: the treatment indicator, one entry per unit.
  • E: N x order exposure columns. Column k is W^k D.
  • order: how many spatial orders E carries.
  • labels: names for the columns of E, e.g. ["WD", "W2D"].
  • standardized: whether W was row-standardized before forming E, which is what makes column 1 the fraction of i's neighbours treated.
The mapping is an assumption, not a measurement

Every spillover estimate is relative to the mapping that produced it. A δ estimated on W built from 5 nearest neighbours answers a different question from one built on a 50 km band, and neither is "the" spillover. Reporting the mapping alongside the estimate is not optional, which is why this type is carried in SpatialDiDResult rather than discarded after the fit.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.exposure — Function
exposure(D, W; order = 1, standardize = true)

Build an ExposureMap from a treatment indicator and a weight matrix: column k of E is W^k D.

Arguments

  • D: treatment indicator, length N. Bool, integer or float. Anything other than 0 and 1 is rejected, because a fractional "treatment" makes the direct/spillover split ill-defined.
  • W: N x N sparse weight matrix.

Keywords

  • order: number of spatial orders. 1 gives WD alone. 2 adds W²D, which lets a second ring differ from the first, instead of being forced to decay at whatever rate W implies.
  • standardize: row-standardize W first. On (the default), WD is the fraction of i's neighbours that are treated, which is bounded in [0, 1] and comparable across units with different neighbour counts. Off, it is a count, and a unit with twenty neighbours is mechanically more "exposed" than one with two.

Examples

em = exposure(D, W)                 # fraction of neighbours treated
em = exposure(D, W; order = 2)      # first and second rings separately

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.ring_exposure — Function
ring_exposure(D, coords, d_min, d_max; order = 1, standardize = true)

Build an ExposureMap from a distance band rather than from an existing W: a unit is exposed through neighbours at distance in (d_min, d_max].

A ring is what separates a spillover from a displacement effect. With d_min = 0 the band includes immediate neighbours, where a policy's effect and its displacement are mixed together. Starting the band beyond them reads the effect at a distance where mechanical displacement is implausible.

Builds the band with band_weights and hands it to exposure.

Examples

near = ring_exposure(D, coords, 0.0, 10.0)    # within 10 units
far  = ring_exposure(D, coords, 10.0, 25.0)   # the next ring out

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.InterferenceReport — Type
InterferenceReport

What an exposure design can and cannot identify, returned by check_interference.

Fields

  • n_treated, n_control: units by treatment status.
  • n_control_unexposed: controls with zero exposure — the units that carry the identification of the spillover.
  • n_treated_unexposed: treated units with no treated neighbour.
  • exposure_var_control: variance of exposure among controls.
  • exposure_cor: correlation between D and first-order exposure.
  • mean_exposure_treated, mean_exposure_control: the two means the reported spillovers are evaluated at.
  • identified: whether the spillover is separable from the time effect.
  • message: what to do about it when it is not.

Why n_control_unexposed is the number to look at

The spillover coefficient is identified by controls that differ in exposure. If every control has a treated neighbour there is no unexposed baseline, and the constant soaks up δ. The fit still returns a number, but it is the difference between more and less exposed, not between exposed and not. If no control has a treated neighbour, δ rests entirely on the treated units' own exposure and cannot be separated from the direct effect at all.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.SpatialDiDResult — Type
SpatialDiDResult

A spatial difference-in-differences fit, returned by fit_spatial_did.

The four effects, and why they are reported apart

FieldMeaning
att_directeffect on a treated unit of its own treatment
att_spill_treatedeffect on a treated unit of its neighbours' treatment
att_spill_controleffect on a control unit of its neighbours' treatment
att_total_treatedatt_direct + att_spill_treated

The two spillovers are evaluated at different exposure means and are not interchangeable. att_spill_control is the policy's reach beyond its target — the quantity a spillover design exists to measure. att_spill_treated is part of what a naive DiD would report as the direct effect, which is the bias this estimator removes.

Each is a fixed linear combination of the coefficient vector, so the standard errors are exact rather than approximations: Var(a'θ̂) = a'Σa.

Fields

  • att_direct, att_spill_treated, att_spill_control, att_total_treated and their *_se counterparts.
  • coef, vcov, coefnames, residuals: the underlying regression.
  • exposure_map: the ExposureMap the spillovers are relative to.
  • interference: the InterferenceReport for that design.
  • vcov_name: which covariance estimator produced vcov.
  • design: :two_period or :staggered.
  • n_units, n_periods.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.fit_spatial_did — Function
fit_spatial_did(y, treat, W; X = nothing, vcov = :cluster,
                coords = nothing,
                dist_cutoff = nothing, order = 1, standardize = true,
                cluster_groups = nothing)

Difference-in-differences with spatial spillovers: decompose a policy effect into what it did to the treated and what it did to their neighbours.

The estimator

With D the treatment, E = W D the exposure and post the policy period, the design is

y_it = μ_i + γ_t + τ·D_i·post_t + δ·E_i·post_t + ε_it

For two periods, first-differencing removes μ_i exactly and leaves an OLS problem in [1, D, E, ΔX], whose coefficients on D and E are τ and δ. For more than two, a two-way within transform does the same job with D_it and E_it varying over time.

A conventional DiD omits E, which does not make the spillover zero. It pushes the spillover into the error, where it is correlated with D by construction, and into the control group, which is no longer untreated. Both biases run through τ̂.

Which spatial DiD this is

This is Delgado and Florax (2015), their eq. (6), read in the paper: y = β₀ + β₁D + β₂T + β₃D·T + β₄(WD)·T + ε. Their derivation carries the restriction β₄ = λβ₃, which this estimator does not impose — D and E enter as free columns — so their restricted case is nested rather than assumed. Their ATE(wd) = β₃ + β₄·wd is what att_total_treated reports.

att_spill_treated and att_spill_control come from one exposure coefficient per order, scaled by the mean exposure of the treated and of the controls. The two differ because the groups have different average exposure, not because two coefficients were estimated.

Chagas, Azzoni and Almeida (2016) eq. (7) is a different estimator and is not implemented here. They split exposure into two channels with separate free coefficients — W_{T,T}b₁ from treated onto treated and W_{NT,T}b₂ from treated onto controls — and their footnote 4 states that "Delgado and Florax (2015) consider only the restricted case". That generalisation is a roadmap row, not a feature of this function. Cite that paper here for the applied design and for the two-channel idea, not for the estimator this code fits.

Arguments

  • y: N x T outcomes, units by periods.
  • treat: either a length-N indicator (two periods, treatment switching on in the second) or an N x T Bool/0-1 matrix giving the full treatment path, which is how a staggered rollout is expressed.
  • W: N x N sparse weight matrix defining exposure.

Keywords

  • X: optional N x T x K array, or N x K matrix for time-invariant covariates, of controls.
  • vcov: :cluster (default), :conley, :ols, or an AbstractRobustVCOV instance. See below — the default is measured, not conventional.
  • coords, dist_cutoff: required by :conley.
  • cluster_groups: required by :cluster. Unit index is the usual choice.
  • order, standardize: passed to exposure.

Which covariance, and why the default is not the spatial one

Coverage of a nominal 95% interval on att_direct, 2,000 replications, N = 144 on a rook lattice with a treated block, errors generated as (I - 0.6W)^{-1}ε. SE/MCsd compares the mean reported standard error with the Monte Carlo standard deviation of the estimate, which is the quantity it is trying to estimate:

EstimatorCoverageSE/MCsd
:cluster (on unit)95.0%1.025
:ols96.5%1.068
:conley, cutoff 1.594.5%1.005
:conley, cutoff 2.592.5%0.948
:conley, cutoff 4.090.5%0.893
:conley, cutoff 6.085.3%0.785

:cluster is the default on that evidence, not on convention. The ordering held across nine configurations: treated block, scattered, half-grid and alternating stripes, at ρ = 0, 0.6 and 0.9, at N = 144 and 400. :cluster stayed within 93.0–97.0%. Conley fell monotonically as the cutoff widened, reaching 82% at the widest.

Conley degrades identically when there is no spatial correlation at all (93.0% → 83.9% across the same cutoffs at ρ = 0). That is the diagnostic that settles what is happening. The decay is the finite-sample cost of estimating more pair covariances, not the correction working. It is not the PSD projection either — psd_fix = false reproduces every figure to three decimals, because the meat is already positive semi-definite here.

Why the spatial correction matters less than it should. The exposure column is what does it. Including WD identifies τ off the contrast between a unit and its neighbours. That contrast differences out the component of the error they share, which is precisely the spatially correlated part. In these designs cor(D, WD) runs from 0.93 to 0.99, and OLS's SE/MCsd barely moves between ρ = 0 and ρ = 0.9 (1.010 to 1.207). In the one design where cor(D, WD) = 0 exactly — alternating stripes — the spatial correlation is orthogonal to the identifying contrast and every estimator over-covers.

So a spatial HAC is still the right instrument for a spatially correlated regressor, and :conley is here for designs where the exposure column does not absorb the dependence. But reaching for it reflexively, at a generous cutoff, makes inference worse than doing nothing. If you use it, report more than one cutoff, and prefer the narrow end.

What clustering on unit is actually doing here

In the two-period design the regression is first-differenced, so there is one observation per unit and each cluster is a singleton. :cluster then reduces to a heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) covariance. That is why it performs well. It is not absorbing serial or spatial correlation, neither of which survives the differencing. In a staggered design with T > 2 the clusters are genuine and it absorbs within-unit serial correlation as usual.

Examples

m = fit_spatial_did(y, D, W; coords = coords, dist_cutoff = 3.0)
m.att_direct, m.att_spill_control

# staggered rollout
m = fit_spatial_did(y, D_path, W; coords = coords, dist_cutoff = 3.0)

# the conventional standard errors, chosen deliberately
m = fit_spatial_did(y, D, W; vcov = :cluster, cluster_groups = 1:N)
Staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects

The two-way fixed effects estimator used when T > 2 does not recover the sample-weighted average treatment effect once effects differ across adoption cohorts. It applies its own implicit weights, some of them negative (Goodman-Bacon). Measured on three-cohort rollouts over eight periods, N = 100, no noise, so every gap below is bias and not sampling error:

Cohort layoutEffect profileTrue ATTatt_direct
contiguous bandsconstant per cohort1.6672.781
contiguous bandsgrowing with exposure4.1671.805
interleavedconstant per cohort1.6622.000
interleavedgrowing with exposure4.1553.440

The error runs from 17% to 67% and its sign is not predictable — too high with constant effects, too low with growing ones.

The spatial dimension makes this worse rather than better, and that is specific to this estimator. When the adoption cohorts are spatially contiguous — which is how rollouts actually happen — cohort identity is nearly collinear with the exposure column, so WD absorbs cohort-specific heterogeneity and att_direct moves to compensate: 1.805 against a true 4.167. With the cohorts spatially interleaved the same profile gives 3.440, and with no exposure column at all, 3.600.

The estimate did stay inside the convex hull of the realised effects in all four designs, but that hull spans [1.0, 8.0] in the dynamic cases, so it is a weak guarantee rather than a reassuring one.

For a staggered design with suspected heterogeneity, estimate cohorts separately and aggregate deliberately. With homogeneous effects this path is exact — testset 34d pins τ and δ to 5e-15 on a noiseless staggered design — so the caveat is about heterogeneity alone, not about the implementation.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.SyntheticControlResult — Type
SyntheticControlResult

The fitted synthetic control for one or more treated units, returned by fit_synthetic_control.

Fields

  • weights: m x J donor weights, one row per treated unit. Each row is non-negative and sums to one.
  • treated, donors: unit indices. donors indexes the columns of weights.
  • excluded_neighbours: untreated units kept out of the donor pool for neighbouring a treated unit. Empty unless donor_pool = :exclude_neighbours did some work.
  • donor_pool_rule: which rule produced donors.
  • synthetic, gaps: m x T. gaps[k, t] is treated unit k's outcome minus its synthetic control at t, so the pre-period entries measure fit and the post-period entries are the estimated effect.
  • att_path: length-T mean gap across treated units.
  • att: mean of att_path over the post-treatment periods.
  • rmspe_pre, rmspe_post, ratio: per treated unit. ratio is rmspe_post / rmspe_pre, the statistic the placebo test ranks.
  • ratio_aggregate: the same ratio formed from att_path.
  • kkt_gap, kkt_violation, iterations: solver diagnostics per treated unit. The two KKT numbers certify the weights are at the constrained optimum and should be ~0. See fit_synthetic_control.

Why there is no vcov

coef, coefnames, nobs and residuals are defined. vcov and stderror are not, and the omission is deliberate. The weights are the solution of a constrained optimisation, not an estimator with a sampling distribution of known form. The gap is a difference between one observed path and one constructed path, rather than a regression coefficient. There is no standard error to report. Inference is the permutation test in placebo_test, with the caveats measured there.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.fit_synthetic_control — Function
fit_synthetic_control(Y, treated, T0; W = nothing, coords = nothing,
                      predictors = nothing, standardize = true,
                      donor_pool = :exclude_neighbours,
                      penalty = 0.0, penalty_metric = :spatial,
                      metric = EuclideanDistance(),
                      tol = 1e-12, maxiter = 20_000)

Synthetic control for a comparative case study, with the donor pool screened for spillover contamination and the donor weights optionally penalised by distance.

The estimator

For treated unit i, weights w over the donor pool solve

minimise   (x_i - X0 w)'(x_i - X0 w) + λ Σ_j d_ij w_j
subject to w ≥ 0,  Σ_j w_j = 1

where x_i collects i's pre-treatment predictors, X0 the donors', and d_ij is the distance from i to donor j. The synthetic control is Σ_j w_j y_jt and the gap y_it - Σ_j w_j y_jt is the estimated effect after T0. With several treated units each gets its own weights and the att is the mean of their post-period gaps.

The program is convex, with a unique minimum whenever X0 has full column rank on the active face. It is solved to its own KKT certificate rather than to an iteration budget. See kkt_gap below.

Why the donor pool is screened

This is the part that makes the method spatial, and it is not cosmetic. If the policy spills over, the treated unit's neighbours are themselves affected. The unpenalised fit will then build the counterfactual out of them, because they track the treated unit best in the pre-period. The spillover then lands inside the comparison and the gap understates the effect. donor_pool = :exclude_neighbours (the default) removes any untreated unit adjacent to a treated one under W, reusing exposure to find them, and records which units went and why.

donor_pool = :all keeps everyone, which is the conventional choice and is wrong whenever the spillover is real. The cost is measured in examples/24_synthetic_control.jl and pinned in the test suite.

Why the penalty

The unpenalised weights are frequently not unique, especially when the donor pool is larger than the pre-period is long — the usual case. Several weight vectors then fit the pre-period identically and imply different counterfactuals, and which one a solver returns is an accident of the algorithm. Abadie & L'Hour (2021) break the tie with a penalty on pairwise discrepancy between the treated unit and each donor it draws on.

penalty_metric = :spatial makes that discrepancy geographic distance. It needs coords, with metric = HaversineDistance() for lon/lat. :covariate uses standardised predictor distance, and :none sets λ = 0. Raising λ moves the solution toward the single nearest donor. λ = 0 is the unpenalised program.

Arguments

  • Y: N x T outcomes, units by periods — the same layout as fit_spatial_did.
  • treated: a unit index, a vector of indices, or a length-N 0/1 or Bool indicator.
  • T0: the last pre-treatment period. Periods T0+1:T are the post-treatment window.

Keywords

  • W: N x N sparse weights, required by the default donor_pool.
  • coords: N x 2, required by penalty_metric = :spatial.
  • predictors: optional N x K pre-treatment predictors. The default is the pre-period outcome path, Y[:, 1:T0].
  • standardize: scale each predictor to unit variance before fitting. This is V = I on standardised predictors, taken deliberately in place of a nested search over V. The nested form is non-unique, so two equally good V give different weights, and the result stops being reproducible.
  • penalty, penalty_metric, metric: as above.
  • tol, maxiter: solver controls.

Fit diagnostics, and reading them honestly

rmspe_pre is the pre-period root mean squared gap. A synthetic control with a poor pre-period fit says nothing about the post period. So ratio = rmspe_post / rmspe_pre is the statistic to look at, rather than the gap alone. A large gap on a badly fitting unit is not evidence. placebo_test ranks exactly this ratio.

kkt_gap and kkt_violation are the solution's own optimality certificate: at the constrained optimum the objective gradient is equal across the donors with positive weight and no smaller on those at zero. Both should be at the 1e-10 level or below. They are reported because unconverged weights are otherwise indistinguishable from converged ones.

Returns

A SyntheticControlResult. Note that it carries no vcov — see that type's docstring for why, and placebo_test for what to use instead.

Example

W = knn_weights(coords, 4)
scm = fit_synthetic_control(Y, 7, 20; W = W, coords = coords,
                            penalty = 0.1)
scm.att                       # mean post-period gap
pt = placebo_test(scm, Y)     # permutation inference
spillover_gaps(scm, Y; W = W, coords = coords)

References

  • Abadie, A., Diamond, A. & Hainmueller, J. (2010). Synthetic control methods for comparative case studies. JASA, 105(490), 493-505.
  • Abadie, A. & L'Hour, J. (2021). A penalized synthetic control estimator for disaggregated data. JASA, 116(536), 1817-1834.
  • Abadie, A. (2021). Using synthetic controls: feasibility, data requirements, and methodological aspects. JEL, 59(2), 391-425.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.placebo_test — Function
placebo_test(scm::SyntheticControlResult, Y; n_placebo = 1000,
             rng = Random.default_rng(), kwargs...)

Permutation inference for a synthetic control: reassign the treatment to units that were never treated, refit, and ask how unusual the real assignment's post/pre RMSPE ratio is.

The statistic is ratio = rmspe_post / rmspe_pre, aggregated across the treated units, and the p-value is

p = (1 + #{ratio_placebo >= ratio_observed}) / (1 + R)

over R placebo assignments. Dividing by the pre-period fit is what makes placebo ratios comparable across units. A donor that the synthetic control tracks badly before treatment will show a large post-period gap for reasons that have nothing to do with any policy.

Y must be the same outcome matrix the fit used. Remaining keyword arguments are forwarded to fit_synthetic_control and must match the original call — W, coords, predictors, penalty and so on — since a placebo fitted under different settings is not a comparison.

Assignments: enumerated or sampled

With J clean donors and m treated units there are binomial(J, m) possible assignments. All of them are enumerated when that number is at most n_placebo, which gives an exact permutation test. Otherwise n_placebo assignments are sampled without replacement using rng. The result records which happened in its exact field.

Placebo units are drawn from the donor pool only. Units excluded for neighbouring a treated unit are not eligible: they are contaminated by the spillover, so a "placebo" there is not a null.

What this p-value cannot do

The smallest attainable value is 1 / (1 + R), and when assignments are enumerated R = binomial(J, m) may be small. A single treated unit with 18 donors cannot produce a p-value below 0.053 however large the effect, so it cannot reject at the 5% level at all. The bound is reported as min_attainable_p rather than left for a user to rediscover.

Permutation inference assumes exchangeable units

The test treats any unit as equally likely to have been treated. Spatial correlation breaks that, and the rejection rate under the null is measured rather than assumed — see the table below and docs/AUDIT.md.

Measured size under the null

Null distribution measured, not assumed: no treatment effect anywhere, a 7x7 rook lattice (N = 49), T = 16 with T0 = 12, an interior treated unit so 4 neighbours are excluded, leaving 44 clean donors. The permutation is therefore exact over 44 assignments and the smallest attainable p-value is 1/45 = 0.0222. Errors generated as (I - rho*W)^{-1}e, 1,000 replications per row.

rhop <= 0.022p <= 0.05p <= 0.10mean p
0.00.0230.0460.0950.503
0.60.0170.0450.0940.492
0.90.0410.0680.1250.438

The test is correctly sized with no spatial correlation and with moderate correlation — a mean p-value of 0.50 is what exchangeability predicts. At rho = 0.9 it over-rejects by roughly a third of the nominal level (6.8% against 5%, 12.5% against 10%, both about 2.6 standard errors above nominal at 1,000 replications). Treat a p-value near the threshold as uninformative when the outcome is strongly spatially correlated.

The ratio statistic is what keeps the distortion this small. Scaling the post-period gap by the pre-period fit makes units comparable even when their error variances are not, so spatial dependence largely divides out.

Returns

A PlaceboTest.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCausal.spillover_gaps — Function
spillover_gaps(scm::SyntheticControlResult, Y; kwargs...)

Estimate the spillover onto the units the donor pool excluded.

fit_synthetic_control with donor_pool = :exclude_neighbours drops every untreated unit adjacent to a treated one, because the spillover contaminates them. That contamination is itself the quantity a spatial policy evaluation wants. This function synthesises each excluded neighbour from the same clean donor pool and reads its gap as the estimated spillover — the synthetic control counterpart of att_spill_control in fit_spatial_did.

The neighbour is not treated, so under no spillover its gap should be noise around zero. A systematic post-period gap is the spillover. The same caution applies as for the main fit: check rmspe_pre before believing a gap, and use placebo_test on the main fit to calibrate what gap sizes arise by chance.

Y must be the outcome matrix the fit used. Remaining keyword arguments are forwarded to fit_synthetic_control and should match the original call.

Errors if the fit excluded no neighbours — there is then nothing to report, and returning an empty result would read as "no spillover" rather than "not asked".

Shift-share designs

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialShiftShare.ShiftShareDesign — Type
ShiftShareDesign

A validated shift-share (Bartik) design: an N × K matrix of exposure shares, a K-vector of shocks, the constructed regressor, and the controls the shares and shocks will be read against. Built by shift_share_design.

The regressor is

X_i = Σ_k W[i, k] * g[k]

so region i's treatment is a weighted average of common shocks, with region-specific weights. That construction is the whole source of this design's difficulty: two regions with similar industry mixes have mechanically correlated values of X, and therefore correlated residuals, whether or not they are anywhere near each other. Inference that assumes independent or merely spatially-clustered regions is not valid here, which is why fit_shift_share defaults to the Adão, Kolesár & Morales standard error rather than a heteroskedasticity-robust one.

Fields

  • W::Matrix{T} — N × K exposure shares, region by shock.
  • g::Vector{T} — the K shocks.
  • X::Vector{T} — the constructed regressor, W * g.
  • Z::Matrix{T} — controls, always including an intercept column first.
  • s_bar::Vector{T} — average exposure to each shock, ŝ_k = (1/N) Σ_i W[i,k].
  • exposure_sum::Vector{T} — per-region Σ_k W[i,k].
  • complete_shares::Bool — whether exposure_sum is constant across regions.
  • shock_names::Vector{String}

References

Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel (2022), Review of Economic Studies 89(1), 181–213.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialShiftShare.shift_share_design — Function
shift_share_design(W, g; controls = nothing, shock_names = nothing)

Validate a shift-share design and construct its regressor.

W is N × K exposure shares (region by shock) and g is the K-vector of shocks. controls is an optional N × L matrix; an intercept is always added and should not be supplied.

Incomplete shares

Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel's framework assumes the shares sum to a constant across shocks for every region. When they do not — the usual case when the shares cover only part of the economy, such as manufacturing employment only — the sum of exposure is itself a regional characteristic that correlates with the regressor, and it must be included as a control. This function reports complete_shares = false and warns once; it does not silently add the control, because whether the exposure sum belongs on the right-hand side is the user's identification decision, not a default.

Errors

Rank-deficient controls, a shock vector of the wrong length, non-finite entries, fewer than two shocks, and an all-zero share column are each refused by name. An all-zero column matters because that shock has no exposure anywhere, so its shock-level aggregate in shock_aggregate would divide by zero.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialShiftShare.fit_shift_share — Function
fit_shift_share(y, design; treatment = nothing, vcov = :akm)

Fit a shift-share regression and attach a standard error that is valid under the design's actual dependence structure.

With treatment = nothing this regresses y on the shift-share regressor and the controls. Supply treatment for the canonical Bartik design, where the shift-share variable instruments an endogenous regional regressor.

Why the default standard error is not the usual one

Adão, Kolesár & Morales (2019) show by placebo that conventional standard errors in this design reject a true null in up to 55% of samples at the 5% level. The reason is structural rather than incidental: residuals are correlated across regions with similar exposure shares, no matter how far apart those regions are, so neither heteroskedasticity-robust nor spatially-clustered covariances address it.

Their estimator (eq. 29) is

se(β̂) = sqrt( Σ_k X̊_k² R̂_k² ) / ( Σ_i X̃_i² ),   R̂_k = Σ_i W[i,k] ε̂_i

and for the IV design (eq. 39) the same numerator over Σ_i X̃_i² · |π̂|, with ε̂ the structural residual and π̂ the first-stage coefficient.

X̊ deserves emphasis because it is easy to get wrong: it is not the shock vector g. It is the vector of coefficients from regressing the residualised regressor X̃ on the share matrix W, following step 2 of their Remark 5. The two coincide only when there are no controls and W is square and invertible.

Inference here is conditional on the shares and on the potential outcomes, with randomness coming from the shocks (their Remark 4). That is why the estimator tolerates arbitrary cross-regional correlation in the residuals.

Keyword vcov

  • :akm (default) — the estimator above.
  • :hc1 — heteroskedasticity-robust, provided for comparison only, so the gap can be seen rather than described. It is the standard error the paper shows to be badly undersized; do not report it.

Requirements

X̊ is identified only when the share matrix has at least as many regions as shocks and is of full column rank. A design with more shocks than regions is refused by name rather than returning a least-norm solution that would look like an answer.

Measured coverage

Coverage of a nominal 95% interval, 2,000 replications, shares and residuals fixed and the shocks resampled (their Remark 4 framework), with N = 300. "Sector frac" is the share of residual variance carried by a sector-mix component — the dependence this estimator exists for. The cluster column clusters on each region's largest-exposure sector.

Three things to take from it. The failure the paper describes is real and large: HC1 rejects a true null in up to 71% of samples here. SpatialEconometrics.SpatialShiftShare.ExposureConcentration — Type
ExposureConcentration

How concentrated average exposure is across shocks, returned by exposure_concentration.

Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel's identification argument needs a Herfindahl condition on average exposure, Σ_k ŝ_k² → 0: no finite set of shocks may drive the estimate asymptotically, which they put equivalently as "the largest industry becomes vanishingly small as the sample grows". The effective_shocks field is the plain reciprocal 1 / Σ_k ŝ_k² of that quantity, reported because a count is easier to read than a sum of squares. It is a rescaling of their condition for legibility and not a named statistic of theirs.

Shares are normalised to sum to one across shocks before the Herfindahl is formed, so that hhi is comparable across designs; with K shocks and uniform exposure, effective_shocks == K exactly.

  • max_share::T — the largest normalised average exposure.
  • order::Vector{Int} — shock indices, most exposed first.
  • top_shares::Vector{T} — the normalised shares in that order.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialShiftShare.ShockAggregates — Type
ShockAggregates

The shift-share design rewritten at the level of the shocks, returned by shock_aggregate.

Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel (2022, eq. 4–5) show that a shift-share estimate is numerically identical to an exposure-weighted estimate computed from K shock-level observations:

β̂ = Σ_k ŝ_k g_k ȳ_k / Σ_k ŝ_k g_k x̄_k

where ŝ_k is average exposure, v̄_k = Σ_i W[i,k] ṽ_i / Σ_i W[i,k] is the exposure-weighted mean of a residualised regional variable, and ȳ, x̄ are those means for the outcome and the regressor or treatment. This is an identity, not an approximation, and beta_equiv reproduces fit_shift_share's estimate to machine precision.

That matters for more than elegance. It says the design has only K independent observations' worth of information, which is why inference must be done at this level — and it makes balance and pre-trend tests straightforward, since conventional standard errors are valid once the regression is a shock-level one. See balance_test.

Fields

  • s_bar::Vector{T}, g::Vector{T} — average exposure and shocks.
  • y_bar::Vector{T} — exposure-weighted means of the residualised outcome.
  • x_bar::Vector{T} — the same for the regressor (direct) or treatment (IV).
  • beta_equiv::T — the ratio above.
  • is_iv::Bool

shock_aggregate(design, y; treatment = nothing) -> ShockAggregates

Collapse the design to shock level, following Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel (2022, eq. 4–5), and return the equivalent estimate.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialShiftShare.balance_test — Function
balance_test(design, r; controls = nothing, names = nothing) -> BalanceTest

Test whether the shocks are balanced with respect to regional characteristics — the exogeneity check a shift-share design actually requires.

r is an N-vector or N × M matrix of regional characteristics that should be unrelated to the shocks if the design is valid: pre-trends, predetermined demographics, anything a reader would worry correlates with the shocks. controls is an optional K × P matrix of shock-level controls.

What is being tested, and why it is at this level

The identifying assumption is quasi-random assignment of the shocks, not of the shares. So the test belongs at shock level: form the exposure-weighted average r̄_k of each residualised characteristic, then regress it on the shock g_k, weighting by average exposure ŝ_k (Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel 2022, §4.3). Conventional heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are valid for that regression, which is the practical payoff of the shock-level equivalence in shock_aggregate.

A test at regional level would be answering a different and largely irrelevant question, because the shares are permitted to be endogenous in this framework.

Reading the result

n_shocks is the real sample size. A balance test over 12 shocks is weak evidence of balance whatever its p-value, and the field is reported so that it cannot be overlooked.

Measured size

Rejection of a true null, 2,000 replications, N = 300, with the characteristic carrying a sector-mix component so that it is structured rather than iid:

The test is correctly sized only when the effective number of shocks is large. At 100 it is almost exact; by 20 it rejects a true null twice as often as it should, and at 9 effective shocks it rejects 23% of the time at a nominal 5%. The joint test degrades faster still, because it estimates an M × M covariance across characteristics from as many observations as there are shocks: with four characteristics and 20 shocks it rejects 32% of the time.

So a passed balance test in a concentrated design is close to uninformative, and a failed one may be the test failing rather than the design. Read exposure_concentration first and treat this test as diagnostic rather than decisive when the effective count is low.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialShiftShare.RotembergWeights — Type
RotembergWeights

The decomposition of a shift-share IV estimate into one just-identified estimate per shock, returned by rotemberg_weights.

Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin & Swift (2020, Proposition 3) show

β̂ = Σ_k α̂_k β̂_k,   β̂_k = (Z̃_k'T̃)⁻¹ Z̃_k'ỹ,
α̂_k = g_k Z̃_k'T̃ / Σ_j g_j Z̃_j'T̃,   Σ_k α̂_k = 1

with Z̃_k the k-th exposure share column, residualised on the controls. Both the unit sum and the decomposition itself hold exactly.

Negative weights are expected, not a defect. The paper says so explicitly: α̂_k can be negative, and under constant effects this poses no conceptual problem. The fields n_negative and negative_mass report them rather than hiding them.

The weights measure sensitivity to misspecification: if shock k is invalid, α̂_k governs how much of that invalidity reaches the overall estimate. The practical use is to report the few shocks carrying the most weight, because those are the ones whose exogeneity has to be defended.

Fields

  • alpha::Vector{T}, beta_k::Vector{T} — weights and just-identified estimates.
  • beta_overall::T — the shift-share IV estimate, Σ_k α̂_k β̂_k.
  • alpha_sum::T — should be 1; carried so the identity is visible.
  • n_negative::Int, negative_mass::T — count and total of negative weights.
  • order::Vector{Int} — shocks by |α̂_k|, largest first.
  • undefined::Vector{Int} — shocks whose first stage is exactly zero, whose β̂_k is NaN and whose weight is zero.

What this does and does not tell you

Spatially correlated exposure shares are the normal case — industries cluster geographically — and finding it is not by itself a problem for a shift-share design, whose identification runs through the shocks. What it does tell you is that any attempt to fix this design's inference with a spatial correction is addressing the wrong dependence: the residual correlation that matters here travels through shared industry mix, and two regions on opposite coasts with the same industries are as correlated as neighbours. Use it to document the spatial structure, and fit_shift_share with vcov = :akm to handle inference.
