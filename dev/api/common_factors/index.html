Common Factors and Spatio-Temporal Models

API reference for SpatialCommonFactors and SpatioTemporalModels. See Common factors for the discussion.

Modules

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors — Module
SpatialCommonFactors

Weak versus strong cross-sectional dependence in spatial panels.

Every panel estimator elsewhere in this package assumes cross-sectional correlation is weak: that it is governed by W, so that conditioning on the spatial lag is enough. The panel literature separates that case from strong dependence driven by unobserved common factors λᵢ'fₜ, which no weight matrix can absorb and which biases ρ̂ when it is ignored.

This module supplies the pair of things that case needs — a test that says which kind of dependence is present, and an estimator that survives the strong kind:

  • pesaran_cd — the CD statistic of Pesaran (2015), and the Breusch–Pagan LM variant it replaces.
  • alpha_exponent — the exponent of cross-sectional dependence of Bailey, Kapetanios & Pesaran (2016), which is what distinguishes weak from strong.
  • dependence_verdict — the two read together, which is the only way to read either.
  • fit_sp_cce — common correlated effects for a spatial panel, augmenting the regression with cross-sectional averages.

Why the pair and not just the test. The CD statistic's null is not independence. Pesaran (2015) shows its implicit null is weak dependence, α < (2−ε)/4 where T = O(Nᵋ) — his eq. (12) — so a non-rejection does not say the units are independent, and a rejection does not say a factor structure is present. The α exponent is the quantity that separates them. Reporting one without the other invites the wrong conclusion in both directions, which is why dependence_verdict exists.

  • Pesaran, M. H. (2015). Testing weak cross-sectional dependence in large panels. Econometric Reviews, 34(6–10), 1089–1117.
  • Bailey, N., Kapetanios, G., & Pesaran, M. H. (2016). Exponent of cross-sectional dependence: Estimation and inference. Journal of Applied Econometrics, 31(6), 929–960.
  • Pesaran, M. H. (2006). Estimation and inference in large heterogeneous panels with a multifactor error structure. Econometrica, 74(4), 967–1012.

  • Ertur, C., & Musolesi, A. (2017). Weak and strong cross-sectional dependence: A panel data analysis of international technology diffusion. Journal of Applied Econometrics, 32(3), 477–503.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatioTemporalModels — Module
SpatioTemporalModels

Models where dependence runs through space and time, and where an unobserved factor structure sits alongside the spatial lag.

Three things live here:

  • fit_star — the space-time autoregression of Pfeifer & Deutsch (1980), yₜ = Σₖ Σₗ φₖₗ W⁽ᵏ⁾ yₜ₋ₗ + Xβ + ε, with arbitrary spatial and temporal lag orders. W⁽ᵏ⁾ is Wᵏ by default and the order-k neighbour matrix — which is what the paper actually defines — under spatial_weights = :orders. The default is the convention the paper does not use; fit_star's docstring says why, and the two coincide for S ≤ 1.
  • fit_interactive_fe — a spatial panel with interactive fixed effects λᵢ'fₜ, by the iterative principal components of Bai (2009) as extended to a spatial lag by Shi & Lee (2017). This is the estimation counterpart to the diagnostics in SpatialCommonFactors.
  • space_time_irf and spatial_forecast — tracing a shock through space and time, and projecting forward.

How STAR differs from the dynamic spatial panel already in the package. STAR's spatial lags are all temporally lagged, so every regressor is predetermined and the model is estimable by least squares. That is the point of the specification: it buys arbitrary lag orders in both dimensions at the cost of ruling out contemporaneous spatial feedback. If you want ρWyₜ — spatial dependence within a period — that is fit_spatial_panel or fit_dynamic_spatial_panel, which pay for it with a Jacobian and a likelihood. The two specifications answer different questions and neither nests the other.

References

  • Pfeifer, P. E., & Deutsch, S. J. (1980). A three-stage iterative procedure for space-time modeling. Technometrics, 22(1), 35–47. Read from the original (NCJRS 60692, the authors' reprint) — see docs/AUDIT.md for what that reading changed here.
  • Anselin, L., & Smirnov, O. (1996). Efficient algorithms for constructing proper higher order spatial lag operators. Journal of Regional Science, 36(1), 67–89.
  • Bai, J. (2009). Panel data models with interactive fixed effects. Econometrica, 77(4), 1229–1279.
  • Shi, W., & Lee, L. F. (2017). Spatial dynamic panel data models with interactive fixed effects. Journal of Econometrics, 197(2), 323–347.
  • LeSage, J., & Pace, R. K. (2009). Introduction to Spatial Econometrics. CRC Press.

Cross-sectional dependence diagnostics

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors.CDTestResult — Type
CDTestResult

Cross-sectional dependence test for panel residuals, returned by pesaran_cd.

Fields

  • stat::Float64 — the statistic, CD or CD_lm per variant.
  • p_value::Float64 — two-sided, from the standard normal.
  • variant::Symbol — :cd or :lm.
  • rho_bar::Float64 — ρ̄_N, the average pairwise residual correlation (Pesaran 2015, eq. 9). The statistic is a scaling of this, and it is the more interpretable of the two: it is bounded in [-1, 1] and says how much dependence there is, where the statistic says only whether it is distinguishable from zero.
  • abs_rho_bar::Float64 — the average absolute pairwise correlation, and the field that rescues the test when the signed average cancels. See "When the signed average cancels" below; under independence it concentrates on sqrt(2/(πT)), which is the number to compare it with.
  • abs_rho_bar_null::Float64 — that benchmark, sqrt(2/(πT)), computed for this T. abs_rho_bar materially above it means dependence is present whatever the signed statistic says.
  • N::Int, T::Int — panel dimensions used.
  • implicit_null_alpha::Float64 — the α threshold below which a non-rejection is consistent with the test's actual null, computed as (2 − ε)/4 with ε = ln(T)/ln(N), from eq. (12). Compare it with alpha_exponent.

Interpretation

A rejection says dependence is stronger than the test's implicit null of weak dependence. It does not identify a factor structure, and a non-rejection does not establish independence. Read it with alpha_exponent via dependence_verdict.

source
SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors.pesaran_cd — Function
pesaran_cd(resid, N, T_per; variant = :cd)

Test the cross-sectional dependence of panel residuals.

resid is a stacked vector of length N*T_per in the package's usual unit-within-time layout, or an N × T_per matrix.

The statistics, as published

With ρ̂ᵢⱼ the pairwise residual correlation (Pesaran 2015, eq. 2),

ρ̂ᵢⱼ = Σₜ eᵢₜ eⱼₜ / [(Σₜ eᵢₜ²)^½ (Σₜ eⱼₜ²)^½]

variant = :cd returns the CD statistic of his eqs. (9) and (10),

ρ̄_N = [2/(N(N−1))] Σᵢ Σⱼ>ᵢ ρ̂ᵢⱼ
CD   = [T N(N−1)/2]^½ ρ̄_N                    →  N(0,1)

and variant = :lm the Breusch–Pagan Lagrange multiplier statistic in the scaled form of his eq. (5),

CD_lm = [1/(N(N−1))]^½ Σᵢ Σⱼ>ᵢ (T ρ̂ᵢⱼ² − 1)   →  N(0,1)

Which variant to use

:cd is the default and is almost always the right choice. CD_lm sums squared correlations, so E(T ρ̂ᵢⱼ² − 1) is not centred at zero in finite T; with N large the mis-centring accumulates across N(N−1)/2 pairs and the test over-rejects. Pesaran (2015, §2.1) gives this as the motivation for CD, and the size distortion is measured in docs/AUDIT.md. :lm is provided to reproduce the comparison, not as a recommendation.

What a result does and does not mean

The CD test's implicit null is weak dependence, not independence — his eq. (12), H₀w : α < (2−ε)/4 where T = O(Nᵋ). So:

  • a non-rejection is consistent with weak dependence, and is not evidence that the units are independent;
  • a rejection says the dependence is stronger than that null, not that it comes from a common factor.

Use alpha_exponent to tell those apart, or dependence_verdict to get both with the applicable thresholds.

When the signed average cancels

ρ̄_N averages signed correlations, and ρ̂ᵢⱼ ∝ λᵢλⱼ under a factor structure, so loadings that are not of one sign do not accumulate. This is measured in docs/AUDIT.md rather than asserted, and it is worse and more specific than "the test loses power" (N = 40, T = 60, 5% level):

| loadings | CD rejection | mean ρ̄_N | mean |ρ̂| | |—|—|—|—| | independent | 0.047 | +0.0000 | 0.1036 | | homogeneous λᵢ ≡ 1 | 1.000 | +0.4961 | 0.4961 | | mean-zero λᵢ ~ N(0,1) | 0.348 | −0.0007 | 0.2929 | | balanced λᵢ = ±1 | 0.911, all negative | −0.0128 | 0.911 |

Two distinct failures, neither of which is a non-rejection:

  1. With random mean-zero loadings power collapses to about a third, and it does not recover as N grows — measured 0.377 at N = 10 and 0.343 at N = 160. It is structural, not a small-sample problem.
  2. With balanced ±1 loadings the test rejects 91% of the time but the statistic is negative, so a strong common factor is reported as negative average dependence. The sign actively misleads.

abs_rho_bar is what survives both. Against the independence threshold it flags 1.000 of mean-zero-loading draws where the signed statistic rejects 0.348. Under independence it concentrates tightly on sqrt(2/(πT)) — measured 0.1036 against a predicted 0.1030 — so abs_rho_bar_null gives it a scale, and show warns when the two diverge. If abs_rho_bar sits well above its benchmark while ρ̄_N is near zero, believe the absolute average.

Example

m = fit_spatial_panel(SAR(), W, X, y, N, T; fe_spec = TwoWayFE())
cd = pesaran_cd(residuals(m), N, T)

References

Pesaran, M. H. (2015). Testing weak cross-sectional dependence in large panels. Econometric Reviews, 34(6–10), 1089–1117. Equations 2, 5, 9, 10 and 12.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors.AlphaExponentResult — Type
AlphaExponentResult

The exponent of cross-sectional dependence, returned by alpha_exponent.

Fields

  • alpha::Float64 — the estimate actually recommended, bias-corrected when bias_correct = true.
  • alpha_raw::Float64 — the baseline estimate of eq. (14), always.
  • se::Float64 — standard error, or NaN when inference is not valid (see inference_valid).
  • ci::Tuple{Float64, Float64} — 95% interval, (NaN, NaN) when inference is not valid.
  • sigma2_xbar::Float64 — σ̂²_x̄, the variance of the cross-sectional average (eq. 13), the quantity everything is computed from.
  • bias_correct::Bool — whether eq. (15)'s correction was applied.
  • kappa2::Float64 — the normalisation used. Misspecifying it shifts alpha by ½ln(κ²)/ln(N); see alpha_exponent.
  • N::Int, T::Int.
  • inference_valid::Bool — false when α̂ ≤ 4/7, where Bailey, Kapetanios & Pesaran's asymptotics do not support inference.

The two thresholds

Both are the paper's, and they are different:

RangeStatus
α ≤ 1/2Not identified. alpha_exponent throws.
1/2 < α ≤ 4/7Identified; inference invalid. se is NaN.
α > 4/7Identified, inference valid.

Interpretation

α is the exponent of N giving the number of units that are pairwise correlated. α = 1 is a strong factor touching every unit; α = 1/2 is the boundary below which the idiosyncratic part dominates and the factor cannot be told from noise. Values near 1 mean W cannot absorb the dependence and a common-factor estimator such as fit_sp_cce is needed.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors.alpha_exponent — Function
alpha_exponent(X, N, T_per; bias_correct = true, standardize = true)

Estimate the exponent of cross-sectional dependence α.

X is a stacked panel vector of length N*T_per (unit-within-time) or an N × T_per matrix — typically a variable of interest or a residual series.

The estimator, as published

With x̄ₜ the cross-sectional average at t, Bailey, Kapetanios & Pesaran (2016) show

Var(x̄ₜ) = κ² N^(2α−2) + N⁻¹ c_N + O(N⁻²)

and invert it. Their eq. (13) estimates the left-hand side,

σ̂²_x̄ = (1/T) Σₜ (x̄ₜ − x̄)²

their eq. (14) gives the baseline estimator

α̂ = 1 + ½ ln(σ̂²_x̄) / ln(N)

and their eq. (15) the bias-corrected one,

α̃ = α̂ − σ̄²_N / (2 N ln(N) σ̂²_x̄),      σ̄²_N = N⁻¹ Σⱼ σ̂²ⱼ

where σ̂²ⱼ is unit j's idiosyncratic variance. standardize = true applies their (xᵢₜ − x̄ᵢ)/sᵢ normalisation, which makes α̂ invariant to the scale each series is measured in — exactly, to 0.0e0, and without it rescaling the units moves α̂ by 0.39 on a measured design.

kappa2, and why it is a keyword rather than 1

Eq. (14) inverts Var(x̄ₜ) = κ² N^(2α−2), so it needs κ² = σ_f² μ_v². BKP are explicit that α is estimated for a given κ², and misspecifying it shifts α̂ by exactly

½ ln(κ²) / ln(N)

This is not a small print detail, and it is measured rather than asserted. On a design whose true κ² is 0.5, at α = 1:

Nbias at κ² = 1predicted ½ln(0.5)/ln Nbias at κ² = 0.5
50−0.0883−0.0886+0.0003
200−0.0668−0.0654−0.0014

The default κ² = 1 is the right convention — it is BKP's own, and it is what makes α̂ comparable across datasets — but it is a normalisation, not a fact about your data. If α̂ looks systematically low, suspect κ² before suspecting the estimator. Note that standardize = true itself changes κ²: dividing each series by its own standard deviation scales the loadings by the signal share, so a panel with equal signal and noise has κ² ≈ 1/2, which is where the table above comes from.

bias_correct

Defaults to true. With κ² correctly specified the eq. (15) correction behaves exactly as BKP describe — it wins in the middle of the admissible range and is neutral at the top (RMSE, 400 reps, T = 100):

true αNbaseline (14)corrected (15)
0.70500.03330.0195
0.702000.02020.0106
0.85500.01600.0119
1.002000.00670.0069

A first pass measured the correction as uniformly worse, which appeared to contradict the paper. It did not: κ² was misspecified at the default, and once that was fixed the published claim reproduced. The episode is in docs/AUDIT.md because the lesson generalises — a measurement that seems to overturn a published result is first evidence about the implementation.

Identification, and why this function throws

α is identified only for α > 1/2. Below that the idiosyncratic contribution to Var(x̄ₜ) is at least as large as a weak factor's, and the two cannot be separated — it is not a precision problem that more data fixes. This function therefore throws rather than returning a number when the estimate falls at or below 1/2: a returned 0.4 would be a value for a parameter that does not exist.

Asymptotically valid inference needs more still, α > 4/7. Between 1/2 and 4/7 the estimate is returned with inference_valid = false and se set to NaN. Their own Monte Carlo reports only α ∈ [0.70, 1.00] for this reason.

Example

a = alpha_exponent(residuals(m), N, T)
a.alpha > 0.9 && @info "strong dependence — W will not absorb this"

References

Bailey, N., Kapetanios, G., & Pesaran, M. H. (2016). Exponent of cross-sectional dependence: Estimation and inference. Journal of Applied Econometrics, 31(6), 929–960. Equations 13, 14 and 15; the α > 1/2 and α > 4/7 thresholds are from their Theorems 1–3 and §5.

source
SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors.dependence_verdict — Function
dependence_verdict(resid, N, T_per; kwargs...)

Report the CD test and the α exponent together, with the threshold that applies to each, and a recommendation.

This exists because neither statistic can be read alone. The CD test's null is weak dependence, not independence (Pesaran 2015, eq. 12), so a non-rejection does not license the usual spatial-panel assumptions by itself; and α is a magnitude, not a test, so it does not say whether the dependence it measures is distinguishable from zero.

kwargs are forwarded to alpha_exponent. When α is not identified — the α ≤ 1/2 case where alpha_exponent throws — that is caught and reported as kind = :unidentified, which in this context is informative rather than a failure: it means the dependence is weak enough that the exponent cannot be separated from noise.

The `:strong` / `:semi_weak` boundary is sensitive to `kappa2`

:strong requires α̂ > 0.9, and α̂ is estimated for a given κ². Under the default kappa2 = 1 the standardisation sets κ² to the signal share instead, biasing α̂ down by ½ln(κ²)/ln(N) — see alpha_exponent. Measured on a panel with a genuine α = 1 factor of moderate strength (N = 60, T = 80, loadings mean 1, sd 0.2, unit noise): α̂ = 0.9093 with sd 0.0109 over 400 draws, so the threshold is 0.85 sd away and 17% of draws are labelled :semi_weak when the truth is α = 1.

The mislabel understates rather than misleads — the :semi_weak recommendation is still to compare fit_spatial_panel against fit_sp_cce and treat a material move in ρ̂ as the factor being absorbed, which is the right action either way. But do not read the boundary as sharp, and pass kappa2 if you can estimate the signal share.

Example

v = dependence_verdict(residuals(m), N, T)
v.kind === :strong && @warn "ρ̂ is absorbing a common factor" v.recommendation

Common correlated effects

SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors.SpCCEModel — Type
SpCCEModel

Spatial panel fitted with common correlated effects, from fit_sp_cce.

Fields

  • rho::T, coef::Vector{T} — spatial parameter and slopes.
  • vcov::Matrix{T} — covariance of [rho; coef], heteroskedasticity-robust.
  • coef_mg::Union{Matrix{T}, Nothing} — unit-specific slopes, one row per unit, when mean_group = true.
  • coef_mg_mean::Union{Vector{T}, Nothing}, vcov_mg::Union{Matrix{T}, Nothing} — the mean-group estimator and its Pesaran (2006) cross-unit dispersion covariance.
  • first_stage_F::T — joint strength of the excluded instruments for Wy. Below about 10 the spatial parameter is weakly identified.
  • sargan::T, sargan_df::Int, sargan_p::T — over-identification test.
  • residuals::Vector{T}, sigma2::T, N::Int, T_periods::Int.
  • augment::Vector{Symbol} — which cross-sectional averages were used.
  • n_augment::Int — columns projected out per unit, the degrees of freedom CCE costs.
  • coefnames::Vector{String}.

What the standard errors assume

The covariance is robust to heteroskedasticity across units and periods. It does not account for the estimation of the factor proxies; Pesaran (2006) shows that cost is O(1/N) and vanishes as the cross-section grows, which is the estimator's premise rather than an oversight. With small N and a strong factor, treat the intervals as optimistic.

source
SpatialEconometrics.SpatialCommonFactors.fit_sp_cce — Function
fit_sp_cce(y, X, W, N, T_per; augment = [:y, :X], mean_group = false,
           fe = NoFE(), coefnames = nothing)

Fit a spatial panel in the presence of unobserved common factors.

Estimates y = ρWy + Xβ + λᵢ'fₜ + ε by purging the factor structure with cross-sectional averages, following Pesaran (2006) as adapted to a spatial lag by Yang (2021).

How it works

At each period the cross-sectional averages of the observables span the space of the unobserved factors as N → ∞. So for every unit i the series yᵢ, (Wy)ᵢ and Xᵢ are projected off

Hₜ = [ȳₜ, X̄ₜ, (W̄y)ₜ]        (whichever `augment` requests)

unit by unit, which is what allows the loadings λᵢ to be heterogeneous — the defining feature of CCE and the reason it is not the same as a time fixed effect.

The spatial lag stays endogenous after projection, so the residualised system is estimated by 2SLS, with instruments [X̃, WX̃, W²X̃]. A likelihood is deliberately not used: projecting off H makes the error covariance non-scalar, so a QML built for spherical errors would be misspecified, and the log-determinant it would buy is not worth a wrong variance.

augment

  • :y — ȳₜ, the cross-sectional average of the outcome.
  • :X — X̄ₜ, the averages of the regressors.
  • :Wy — (W̄y)ₜ. Usually redundant: with a row-standardised W the average of Wy is close to the average of y, so adding it buys a nearly collinear column. Offered for the cases where W is not row-standardised.

The default [:y, :X] is Pesaran's. Each requested average costs one projected column per unit, reported as n_augment, so augment is not free: with T small the projection can exhaust the time variation.

Time fixed effects are redundant here

ȳₜ is already a time-varying intercept, so the augmentation block spans the time effects. Passing fe = TimeFE() or TwoWayFE() therefore removes variation the projection would remove anyway, and is accepted but warned about. IndivFE() is not redundant and composes normally.

When you need this, and when TwoWayFE is better

TwoWayFE is not a weaker version of CCE, and the choice is not "more robust is safer". What a time effect can reach is the mean of the loadings, so everything turns on their distribution. Measured bias and RMSE for ρ̂ at a true ρ = 0.4, N = 42, T = 30, 200 reps, with a factor present in every design:

loadingsNoFETwoWayFEfit_sp_ccefit_interactive_fe
mean 2 (dominant)+0.484+0.002 / 0.029−0.001 / 0.047−0.032 / 0.041
mean 1+0.285+0.003 / 0.068−0.000 / 0.047−0.017 / 0.032
mean 0, random−0.004+0.003 / 0.068−0.008 / 0.059−0.002 / 0.025
mean 0, balanced ±1+0.329+0.373 ⚠+0.005 / 0.100−0.023 / 0.035

Three things follow, none of them obvious:

  1. With a dominant common loading, TwoWayFE wins outright — RMSE 0.029 against CCE's 0.047. It is the correctly specified parsimonious model there, and CCE pays N·q projected columns for nothing.
  2. With balanced signed loadings, TwoWayFE is worse than doing nothing (+0.373 against NoFE's +0.329). Removing the cross-sectional mean when the mean is already zero destroys variation and leaves the factor untouched. This is the configuration CCE exists for.
  3. That same configuration is the one where both published diagnostics fail — pesaran_cd's power falls to about a third and alpha_exponent refuses as unidentified. The quantity that does detect it is abs_rho_bar; see pesaran_cd.

So: check abs_rho_bar against its benchmark before choosing. If the loadings look dominant and one-signed, TwoWayFE is the better estimator; if they cancel, it is actively harmful and this function is what you want.

Example

m  = fit_spatial_panel(SAR(), NoFE(), W, X, y, N, T)   # ignores the factor
mc = fit_sp_cce(y, X, W, N, T)                         # purges it
abs(m.rho - mc.rho) > 2 * stderror(mc)[1] &&
    @warn "ρ̂ was absorbing a common factor"

References

  • Pesaran, M. H. (2006). Econometrica, 74(4), 967–1012.
  • Yang, C. F. (2021). Econometric Reviews, 40(1), 14–50.

Space-time autoregression

SpatialEconometrics.SpatioTemporalModels.STARModel — Type
STARModel

A fitted space-time autoregression, from fit_star.

Fields

  • phi::Matrix{T} — the autoregressive coefficients, (S+1) × L, with phi[k+1, l] multiplying Wᵏ yₜ₋ₗ. Row 1 is k = 0, the purely temporal lags.
  • beta::Vector{T} — exogenous slopes, excluding the intercept.
  • intercept::T, has_intercept::Bool — the intercept is kept apart from beta so that length(beta) == size(X, 2) always holds; intercept is zero when has_intercept is false.
  • coef::Vector{T}, vcov::Matrix{T}, coefnames::Vector{String} — the flattened parameter vector and its covariance, phi first in column order then beta.
  • sigma2::T, loglik::T, residuals::Vector{T}.
  • spatial_lags::Int (S), temporal_lags::Int (L).
  • N::Int, T_periods::Int — the original panel; T_eff = T − L periods are actually used.
  • W::SparseMatrixCSC{T, Int} — kept for space_time_irf and spatial_forecast.
  • stationary::Bool — whether the companion form's spectral radius is below one. When false the fit is still reported but forecasts and impulse responses will diverge, and both functions say so.
  • spectral_radius::T.

Why least squares is the right estimator here

Every regressor is dated t−1 or earlier, so none is correlated with εₜ and OLS is consistent without a Jacobian. This is the defining advantage of the STAR parameterisation over a contemporaneous spatial lag, and the reason arbitrary lag orders are affordable.

source
SpatialEconometrics.SpatioTemporalModels.fit_star — Function
fit_star(y, X, W, N, T_per; spatial_lags = 1, temporal_lags = 1,
         spatial_weights = :powers, varnames = nothing,
         intercept = true)

Fit the space-time autoregression of Pfeifer & Deutsch (1980).

yₜ = Σ_{k=0}^{S} Σ_{l=1}^{L} φₖₗ W⁽ᵏ⁾ yₜ₋ₗ + Xₜβ + εₜ

spatial_lags = S and temporal_lags = L set the two orders. W⁽⁰⁾ = I, so φ₀ₗ are the plain temporal autoregressive coefficients and the model nests a non-spatial panel AR(L) at S = 0.

What W⁽ᵏ⁾ is, and why the default is not the paper's

spatial_weights chooses the sequence:

valueW⁽ᵏ⁾
:powers (default)Wᵏmatrix powers
:ordersthe order-k neighbour matrixPfeifer & Deutsch's own definition

Pfeifer & Deutsch define a spatial lag operator L⁽ᵏ⁾ z = W⁽ᵏ⁾ z in which wᵢⱼ⁽ᵏ⁾ is "nonzero only if sites i and j are kth order neighbors" (their Section 2), each W⁽ᵏ⁾ row-standardised separately, and the orders required to form a hierarchy — successively more distant rings, drawn as such in their Figure 1 for a lattice and a line. That is order_weights, not Wᵏ.

Wᵏ is a different object: it counts walks of length k, is non-zero on its own diagonal, double-counts paths, and keeps first-order neighbours wherever the graph has triangles. The two agree at k = 0 and k = 1 and diverge from k = 2.

The default is :powers anyway, because that is what this function has always computed and what its tests pin. A citation being read more carefully is not a reason to change the arithmetic under a user who has shipped results; :orders is the paper's model and is here for anyone who wants it. The distinction is invisible at the default spatial_lags = 1. Coefficient names tell you which you got: W^2 y(t-1) versus W⁽²⁾ y(t-1).

y is a stacked panel vector of length N*T_per in the package's unit-within-time layout; X has N*T_per rows. The first L periods are consumed by the lags, leaving T_eff = T_per − L.

Estimated by least squares, deliberately

All regressors are dated t−1 or earlier, so the model has no simultaneity and OLS is consistent. There is no contemporaneous Wyₜ term, and adding one would change the estimator entirely — see fit_spatial_panel for that specification. The trade is explicit: STAR buys arbitrary lag orders in both dimensions by giving up within-period spatial feedback.

Identification

W⁽⁰⁾ = I, W⁽¹⁾, …, W⁽ˢ⁾ must be linearly independent as matrices, or the design has exactly collinear blocks. Under :powers on a row-standardised W with a regular structure this fails for modest S — a complete graph gives W² ∝ W, for instance. The design is checked with check_design_rank and the error names the offending columns rather than failing inside LAPACK. Under :orders the binding constraint is different and caught earlier: once S exceeds the graph's diameter some W⁽ᵏ⁾ is empty, and order_weights_list refuses with the diameter rather than handing back a column of zeros.

Example

m = fit_star(y, X, W, N, T; spatial_lags = 2, temporal_lags = 1)
irf = space_time_irf(m; horizon = 12)
f   = spatial_forecast(m, X_future; h = 4)

InteractiveFEModel

A spatial panel with an estimated factor structure, from fit_interactive_fe.

Fields

  • rho::T, beta::Vector{T}, vcov::Matrix{T} — covariance of [rho; beta].
  • factors::Matrix{T} — T × r, normalised so F'F/T = I_r.
  • loadings::Matrix{T} — N × r.
  • n_factors::Int, sigma2::T, loglik::T, residuals::Vector{T}. sigma2 is degrees-of-freedom corrected, dividing by NT − k − 1 − r(N+T−r), and is what vcov is built from. The likelihood uses the ML variance SSR/NT, as the concentrated profile requires, so loglik and sigma2 are not two views of one number.
  • iterations::Int, converged::Bool, final_change::T — the iterative principal-components loop's record.
  • N::Int, T_periods::Int, coefnames::Vector{String}.

What is identified, and what is not

F and Λ are identified only up to an r × r rotation: for any invertible R, (FR, ΛR⁻ᵀ) fits identically. So factors and loadings are one particular representative, fixed by the normalisation F'F/T = Iᵣ, and comparing them across fits is meaningless. What is identified is the space they span, i.e. the projection F(F'F)⁻¹F' — use factor_projection to get it, and compare that. rho, beta and the fitted values are of course identified too.

fit_interactive_fe(y, X, W, N, T_per; n_factors = 1, fe = NoFE(),
                   maxiter = 500, tol = 1e-10, n_starts = 1,
                   rho_bounds = (-0.995, 0.995), varnames = nothing,
                   rng = Random.default_rng())

Fit y = ρWy + Xβ + λᵢ'fₜ + ε by iterative principal components.

This is the estimator that pairs with pesaran_cd and alpha_exponent in SpatialCommonFactors: those say whether a factor structure is present, this one estimates it jointly with the spatial parameter so that ρ̂ is not left absorbing a global shock.

How it works

ρ is profiled out on a one-dimensional grid search (Brent), and at each trial ρ the Bai (2009) loop runs on z = y − ρWy:

  1. β given F — least squares after projecting the factor space off every series, β = (Σᵢ Xᵢ'M_F Xᵢ)⁻¹ Σᵢ Xᵢ'M_F zᵢ with M_F = I_T − F(F'F)⁻¹F'.
  2. F given β — the top r eigenvectors of R'R, where R is the residual panel, normalised so F'F/T = Iᵣ.

The concentrated log-likelihood adds the usual Jacobian, T·log|I − ρW|, computed exactly by sparse LU — the Chebyshev approximation is deliberately not offered here, because the profile is compared across ρ values and an approximation error that varies with ρ would move the optimum.

fe composes normally: additive unit and time effects are swept by the within transform before the factor loop, which is Bai's own §3 ordering. Note that TimeFE is close to redundant — a factor with homogeneous loadings is a time effect — but they are not the same thing, and the difference is the whole point of this estimator.

n_factors is a choice, and it matters asymmetrically

There is no automatic selection here. Over-specifying r costs efficiency; under-specifying it leaves factor structure in the error, which is the failure mode that biases ρ̂. The measured cost of each direction is in docs/AUDIT.md. When in doubt, and having checked alpha_exponent, prefer one too many to one too few.

n_factors = 0 reduces the estimator to a spatial panel with no factor structure, which is how it is tested against fit_spatial_panel.

Local optima

Bai's loop is not globally convex. n_starts > 1 runs it from several random factor initialisations and keeps the best log-likelihood; the measured sensitivity is documented in docs/AUDIT.md. A non-converged fit is reported as such in converged and by show rather than passed off as a fixed point.

Example

v = dependence_verdict(residuals(fit_spatial_panel(SAR(), NoFE(), W, X, y, N, T)), N, T)
if v.kind === :strong
    m = fit_interactive_fe(y, X, W, N, T; n_factors = 1)
end

Fields

The summaries follow LeSage & Pace's direct/indirect/total decomposition, read at each horizon rather than only in the long run.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatioTemporalModels.space_time_irf — Function
space_time_irf(m::STARModel; horizon = 10, cumulative = false,
               store_matrices = true)

Trace a one-period shock through space and time.

The reduced form is yₜ = Σₗ Aₗ yₜ₋ₗ + … with Aₗ = Σₖ φₖₗ Wᵏ, so the response matrices satisfy

Ψ₀ = I,    Ψₕ = Σ_{l=1}^{min(h,L)} Aₗ Ψₕ₋ₗ

and Ψₕ[i, j] is the effect on unit i at horizon h of a unit shock to unit j at horizon 0. The summaries decompose each horizon into an own effect (the diagonal) and a spillover (everything else), in the spirit of LeSage & Pace's direct/indirect split but resolved by horizon.

The long run

With cumulative = true the sums converge to (I − Σₗ Aₗ)⁻¹, the long-run multiplier — which is the identity the implementation is tested against. If the model is not stationary the sums diverge instead, and the result says so rather than returning a plausible-looking number.

store_matrices = false keeps only the summaries, for a large N where horizon copies of an N × N matrix is too much memory.

SpatialEconometrics.SpatioTemporalModels.spatial_forecast — Function
spatial_forecast(m::STARModel, X_future; h = 5, y_history = nothing)

Forecast h periods ahead from a fitted STAR model.

X_future has N*h rows in the usual unit-within-time layout, giving the exogenous regressors over the forecast horizon. The recursion is the model's own,

ŷₜ₊ₛ = Σₗ Aₗ ŷₜ₊ₛ₋ₗ + Xₜ₊ₛβ + intercept

with realised values used where available and forecasts substituted thereafter — a dynamic, not a static, forecast.

y_history supplies the last L periods to start from, as an N*L vector. It defaults to the tail of the data the model was fitted on, which is reconstructed from the residuals, so passing it explicitly is the safer course when forecasting from new data.

Returns an N × h matrix. A non-stationary fit will produce diverging numbers and emits a warning rather than failing, because the divergence is the honest answer for those parameters.
