Model Selection & Comparison

Information Criteria

spatial_aic(m)  # Akaike Information Criterion
spatial_bic(m)  # Bayesian Information Criterion

Likelihood Ratio Test

For nested models (e.g., SAR nested in SDM):

lr = lr_test(m_restricted, m_unrestricted)
# lr.stat — LR statistic (χ²)
# lr.df — degrees of freedom
# lr.p_value — reject H₀ → unrestricted model preferred

Vuong Test

For non-nested models (e.g., SAR vs SEM):

vt = vuong_test(m1, m2)
# vt.stat — Vuong statistic (positive favors m1)
# vt.preference — :model1, :model2, or :indistinguishable

Common Factor Test

Tests whether SDM simplifies to SEM (H₀: θ + ρβ = 0):

cft = common_factor_test(beta_slope, theta, rho, vcov_full)
# cft.reject — true → SDM preferred over SEM

Wald Test

General linear hypothesis Rβ = r:

R = zeros(1, length(coef(m)))
R[1, 2] = 1.0  # test H₀: β₁ = 0
wt = wald_test(m, R, [0.0])

Comparison Table

tbl = model_comparison_table(m_sar, m_sdm, m_sem; names=["SAR","SDM","SEM"])
println(tbl)

Bayesian specification comparison

Everything above compares models you have already fitted, on a W you have already chosen. bayes_spec_probs compares the specifications — including ones of different width, and including the choice of W — by integrating each one's likelihood over its parameters instead of maximising it.

ev = bayes_spec_probs(y, X, [W_rook, W_queen]; w_names = ["rook", "queen"])
println(ev)
# ev.prob         — the posterior over the (specification, W) grid
# ev.prob_spec    — its specification margin
# ev.prob_w       — its weight-matrix margin
# ev.prob_global  — mass on :sar and :sdm, spillovers running through y
# ev.prob_local   — mass on :slx, :sem and :sdem, spillovers in the errors

The six specifications, their designs, and where each puts the dependence:

specDesignSpatial parameterSpillovers
:ols[1 X]—none
:slx[1 X WX]—local, through W*X
:sar[1 X]ρ on yglobal
:sdm[1 X WX]ρ on yglobal
:sem[1 X]λ on ulocal
:sdem[1 X WX]λ on ulocal

Global against local is the substantive question, and it is not a nesting question: :sdm and :sdem have the same design and the same number of parameters, and no likelihood-ratio test runs between them. prob_global and prob_local answer it directly.

What is integrated, and what is not

Under the improper prior p(β, σ²) ∝ 1/σ² the inner integral is exact, leaving one dimension to quadrature:

\[p(y \mid \rho) = |A(\rho)| \; \pi^{-(n-k)/2} \; |Z'Z|^{-1/2} \; \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n-k}{2}\right) \; S(\rho)^{-(n-k)/2}\]

A(ρ) is I - ρW for a lag specification and I - λW for an error one, Z is the design, and S is the residual sum of squares at that value of the parameter. The |Z'Z|^{-1/2} and Γ((n-k)/2) factors are what make a wider design pay for its width, so they are kept rather than dropped as constants: dropping them still ranks :sar against :sem correctly and mis-ranks every comparison that crosses a k.

For an error specification both S and |Z'Z| are evaluated on the filtered data (I - λW)[y Z], so the determinant term moves with λ. It is constant for a lag specification, which is why leaving it out is an easy mistake and a silent one — it biases the comparison toward the error specifications, by an amount that grows with |λ̂|.

The outer integral is a trapezoid rule over rho_bounds, evaluated by log-sum-exp because the integrand spans hundreds of orders of magnitude. Where the posterior rises and falls away inside the support the rule is geometrically accurate and 100 nodes is already at machine precision; where it is still appreciable at the edge — a misspecified error model wanting λ → 1, or any specification at very small N — the rate drops to the generic O(h²) and the error at the default 200 nodes is around 1e-4, three orders below the gaps that separate specifications.

The prior does not wash out

A marginal likelihood is not a likelihood ratio. The prior sits on a parameter that :ols and :slx do not have, so widening rho_bounds spreads it thinner and penalises every spatial specification by exactly the width ratio. Report the setting next to the probabilities, and if a comparison is close, run it under prior = :beta as well. Nothing about increasing N removes this.

How often it finds the generating specification

A selection procedure's gate is its error rate, so: 200 draws from each of the six specifications on a rook lattice, ρ = λ = 0.5, θ = (0.7, 0), σ = 0.5, with all six compared on the generating W under the default uniform prior. The entry is the share of draws whose highest posterior probability lands on the generating specification.

N:ols:slx:sar:sdm:sem:sdemreach right
10093.0%90.0%81.5%95.5%88.5%88.5%90.5–100%
25692.5%96.0%94.0%100%96.5%96.0%96.0–100%
40094.5%94.0%97.5%100%96.5%98.5%98.5–100%

Chance is 16.7%. The mean posterior probability assigned to the generating specification runs 0.589–0.916 at N = 100 and 0.711–0.999 at N = 256, so the procedure is not merely picking the right winner — it is reasonably confident about it, and more so as N grows.

The last column is the share of draws that get the reach right — mass on global exceeding mass on local when the data are :sar or :sdm, and the other way round for :slx, :sem and :sdem. That coarser question is answered more reliably than the full six-way choice at every N, which is what you would expect, and it is usually the question the application is actually asking.

The confusions are the ones the structure predicts, and they are not all inside one reach. :ols loses draws to :sem, which nests it at λ = 0, and to :slx, which nests it at θ = 0. :sar loses them to :sdm — the same reach, one Durbin block apart — and, at N = 100, to :sdem in 10.0% of draws, which is a crossing. :sdem loses 9.0% to :sdm at N = 100, the mirror image: the two have identical designs and differ only in where the dependence sits, so they are the hardest pair in the grid and the one the reach columns are measuring. Every crossing in the table is between specifications carrying the same Durbin block; at N = 400 the worst of them is down to 1.5%.

The SDEM roadmap

sdem_roadmap is the local-spillover search of Sutton & Sutton (2023): for each candidate W, decide which of :sdem, :slx and :sem fits best, then read the winner across the candidates.

rm = sdem_roadmap(y, X, [W_rook, W_queen, W_knn4];
                  w_names = ["rook", "queen", "knn4"])
println(rm)
# rm.best_spec — the winner on each W
# rm.margin    — the gap to the runner-up on that W
# rm.best_w, rm.best_overall — the winning cell of the grid

The default criterion is the log marginal likelihood, because it is the only one of the three on offer that integrates over the spatial parameter rather than plugging in an estimate, and the specifications being compared differ in whether they have that parameter. :aic and :bic are available and are minimised rather than maximised; sdem_roadmap reads them in the right direction, and the three need not agree.

margin is the point of returning a table rather than a winner. The choice of W and the choice of specification are decided together, and a margin near zero says the data do not separate them — which is a result to report, not a tie to break by taking the maximum.

The roadmap is the local case

:sar and :sdm put the dependence on y. Pass them through specs if you want them in the same table, but the global-versus-local comparison is what bayes_spec_probs is for; the roadmap holds the reach fixed and searches within it.

Choosing k for a knn W

Everything above compares specifications on a W you have already chosen. select_knn_w chooses the W — specifically k, when W comes from knn_weights — by refitting across a range of candidates and minimising an information criterion.

sel = select_knn_w(coords, y, X; k_range = 2:25)   # X without an intercept
println(sel)                                        # the curve and its winner
m = fit_spatial(SAR(), sel.best_W, X, y)            # refit on the chosen W

The criterion is not monotone in k, which is why there is something to search: a W that is too sparse misses real neighbours, and one that is too dense dilutes them. On a 200-unit draw generated from a knn W at k = 6, AIC falls from 312.7 at k = 2 to 294.3 at k = 6 and then climbs to 329.1 at k = 20.

k matters because ρ̂ moves with it, monotonically and over a wide range — 0.305 at k = 2, 0.482 at the true k = 6, 0.630 at k = 20, against a true 0.5 on that same draw. Too sparse understates the dependence; too dense overstates it.

How often the search finds the generating k, over 300 draws with k searched over 2:20 (so 5.3% by chance):

Nk̂ = 6within ±1within ±2
10042.3%68.3%84.0%
20057.3%86.0%95.7%
40077.0%95.0%99.7%

The median k̂ is 6 at every N. Read that as the search being well targeted but not sharp: at N = 100 it misses the exact k more often than it finds it, and the honest use of the result is the whole curve plus a sensitivity check, not the argmin alone. KnnSelectionResult returns every candidate's criterion for that reason.

AIC is not penalising anything here

With the variant and X held fixed, the number of estimated parameters does not change with k, so AIC, BIC and the raw log-likelihood rank the candidates in exactly the same order. The criterion is reported so the numbers line up with spatial_aic elsewhere; its penalty only starts to matter once the comparison runs across variants as well.

Warning

A candidate whose fit throws, or whose ρ̂ lands on the edge of rho_bounds, is marked converged = false, given a NaN criterion and excluded from the argmin — a failed fit must not win by having no number to beat. The criteria are only comparable because y and the sample are identical across candidates, which select_knn_w checks rather than assumes.
