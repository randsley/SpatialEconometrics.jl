Spatial log-GARCH

Every other model in the package puts spatial dependence in the conditional mean. fit_spatial_log_garch puts it in the conditional variance: a unit's volatility depends on its neighbours' volatility and on their squared outcomes. It implements the two-step estimator of Sato & Matsuda (2021).

The model

For a cross-section of mean-zero data y (returns, or the residuals of a mean model):

\[y_i = \sqrt{h_i}\,ε_i, \qquad \log h = λW\log h + ρW\log y^2 + α1_n + Zδ,\]

with ε_i i.i.d., E ε_i² = 1, and one row-standardised W in both places. Z holds optional exogenous variables. Write Y = log y², V = log ε² − E log ε² and R(x) = I − xW. Then the model is a spatial ARMA(1,1) in Y:

\[R(λ + ρ)\,Y = Xβ + R(λ)\,V, \qquad X = [1_n, Z],\quad β = (α + (1 − λ)c,\ δ),\]

where c = E log ε². A stationary solution needs |λ + ρ| < 1. Rook contiguity, as in Sato & Matsuda's simulations:

W = make_rook_weights(20)                    # 400 units
m = fit_spatial_log_garch(y, W; Z = z)
m.lambda, m.rho, m.alpha, stderror(m)
m.log_volatility                             # fitted log ĥ

What separates λ from ρ

The mean of Y depends on λ and ρ only through λ + ρ. λ enters on its own only through the moving-average error R(λ)V. So λ + ρ is estimated well and λ poorly, and their estimates are strongly negatively correlated. With an intercept and no Z, the error part is all that identifies λ.

Sato & Matsuda assume identification (their Assumption 3.8) and do not state this. The Monte Carlo below measures what it costs.

No interior maximum near λ = 1

The quasi log-likelihood has no upper bound as λ → 1, in every sample:

  • W is row-standardised, so W1 = 1 and R(λ)1 = (1 − λ)1;
  • R(λ)⁻¹ inflates only the part of R(λ + ρ)Y − Xβ along the eigenvalue 1, and the intercept in β can set that part to zero, so V and σ̂² stay bounded;
  • meanwhile −log|R(λ)| grows like −log(1 − λ).

The estimate is therefore an interior local maximum, not the global one. The test suite checks the divergence value against value: the concentrated log-likelihood rises by log 10 for each decade of 1 − λ, and σ̂² settles.

When a sample has no interior maximum, the search runs to the edge of bounds and the fit throws an error that says so. It does not return the edge point. With rook W, (λ, ρ) = (0.9, 0.05) and n = 100, this happens in 36–39% of samples, and not at all at n = 400. Sato & Matsuda do not mention it, and their Table 1 reports all 1000 samples.

Estimation

Step 1 is Gaussian quasi maximum likelihood for the SARMA form (Sato & Matsuda eqs 7–9). β and σ² = Var V are profiled out, which leaves a two-parameter search in θ = (λ, λ + ρ):

  • a 21 × 21 grid inside bounds (default (−0.99, 0.99) for both);
  • then Newton steps on the analytic score and Hessian, with step halving, until max|∂ℓ/∂θ|/n < tol (default 1e-8).

If that tolerance is not reached, the fit throws. It never returns a point that does not solve the first-order conditions.

log|R(x)| and the traces in the score come from the eigenvalues of W. They are computed once per fit, at O(n³) cost, or passed in with eigenvalues when many fits share one W. A fit takes about 0.1 s at n = 100, 0.3 s at 400 and 1.7 s at 1600.

Step 2 recovers the volatility intercept from E ε² = 1 (eq. 11):

\[\hat α = (1 − \hat λ)\log\Big(n^{-1}\sum_i \exp C_i\Big), \qquad C = R(\hat λ)^{-1}\big(R(\hat λ + \hat ρ)Y − Z\hat δ\big).\]

Sato & Matsuda prove α̂ consistent (Theorem 3.3) but give it no distribution, so it has no standard error here.

Standard errors

log ε² is far from normal: with normal ε it is log χ²₁, with skewness −2.1 and excess kurtosis 4. The Gaussian information −Ĥ⁻¹ is therefore wrong, and Sato & Matsuda's Theorem 3.2 gives a sandwich instead. Its middle term, the score covariance Ω, is not written out in the paper or its supplement, so it is derived here.

At the true parameters every score component is a linear-quadratic form in the i.i.d. V:

\[S_k = a_k'V + V'A_kV − σ^2\operatorname{tr}A_k .\]

For example, S_λ = −V'GV/σ² + tr G with G = R(λ)⁻¹W. Such forms have an exact covariance in σ² and the third and fourth moments μ₃, μ₄ of V:

\[\operatorname{Cov}(S_i, S_j) = 2σ^4 \operatorname{tr}(A_iA_j) + (μ_4 − 3σ^4)\sum_k A_{i,kk}A_{j,kk} + μ_3\sum_k (A_{i,kk}a_{j,k} + A_{j,kk}a_{i,k}) + σ^2 a_i'a_j .\]

The reported vcov is Ĥ⁻¹Ω̂Ĥ⁻¹, where:

  • Ĥ is the analytic Hessian;
  • Ω̂ is the formula above at the estimates, with σ̂², μ̂₃ and μ̂₄ taken from the step-1 residuals.

vcov_gaussian keeps −Ĥ⁻¹ for comparison. It is right only when log ε² is normal, which happens when ε is log-normal.

The moments are printed by show, so the departure from normality is visible in every fit.

How it is checked

The test suite (testset 44) compares values with values:

  • the quasi log-likelihood with a dense computation, and the eigenvalue log-determinants and traces with logabsdet and tr;

  • the analytic score and Hessian with automatic differentiation, at arbitrary parameters;

  • the linear-quadratic forms with the score at the true parameters, and the moment formula with a brute-force sum over all index quadruples;

  • Var(S(ψ₀)) against the exact formula, by simulation, under three error laws:

    • normal ε;
    • ε² ~ Gamma(2)/2;
    • log-normal ε.

    The design is held fixed, and the largest |z| over the 15 entries is 1.9 on Julia 1.13 and 1.7 on 1.10. E[Ω̂(ψ₀)] with sample moments is tested the same way (largest |z| 0.6 and 1.2). The Gaussian information fails the same test with |z| = 30 and 29 when ε is normal, which shows the test has power;

  • α̂ and log ĥ recomputed densely, the sandwich rebuilt from its parts, and invariance to the order of the units.

Monte Carlo

validation/mc_spatial_log_garch.jl runs three designs. Its output is validation/mc_spatial_log_garch_output.txt. A fit that throws is counted; the summaries are over the fits that return. ε is scaled so that E ε² = 1:

  • normal;
  • χ²₃/√15;
  • log-normal exp(Z − 1).

What the cells show:

  • The SEs have the right scale. Mean SE / sd is 0.95–1.04 in every cell at n = 400.
  • At (0.9, 0.05), the intervals for λ and ρ cover 87–89%. The SE scale is right there too, and the bias is 0.3–0.45 of an sd. With that bias and SE scale, a normal sampling distribution would give .92–.94 coverage, not the .87–.89 observed. The shortfall comes from the shape of the sampling distribution, not from the variance. This is the design closest to the edge where the likelihood is unbounded.
  • At n = 100 with (0.9, 0.05), 355, 368 and 390 of 1000 fits throw (normal, χ²₃, log-normal), every one at the edge of bounds. The fits that return have a λ RMSE of .050, against Sato & Matsuda's .082 over all samples. At (0.45, 0.45) and n = 100, one fit in 1000 throws under each law, also at the edge.
  • The sandwich matters at (0.9, 0.05). There the Gaussian intervals for λ and ρ cover .79–.88, against .91–.96 for the sandwich at n = 100, and .84–.87 against .87–.89 at n = 400. In every other cell of A and B the two coverages differ by at most .02, in either direction, even though the score covariances differ widely (testset 44d). Why the gap stays this small for δ, λ and ρ was not measured.
  • At n = 100 with (0.05, 0.9), the RMSEs of λ̂ and ρ̂ are larger than Sato & Matsuda's: .187 and .155 against .139 and .108 with normal ε. By n = 400 they agree to within .007. A diagnostic run rules out the starting point and the draw of z. Restricting λ ≥ 0 moves every figure towards theirs and past it: the unrestricted λ̂ is negative in about half the samples when λ₀ = 0.05. So the most likely cause is a narrower search region, which the paper does not state. The package keeps the unrestricted region (details in docs/AUDIT.md).
  • α̂ errs most when λ is small and ε heavy-tailed, as Sato & Matsuda find. With log-normal ε and (0.05, 0.9), its bias is −1.09 at n = 100 and −0.28 at n = 400 (theirs: −1.01 and −0.31). With (0.9, 0.05) it is at most .02 in absolute value.

At a fixed noise level the proxy gets worse as n grows, because the points crowd together while the noise does not shrink. ρ̂ is then biased towards zero while its sd stays at .04–.06, and coverage collapses: at n = 1600 and ω = 0.1, the proxy keeps 6% of the true neighbours and the ρ interval covers 11% of the time. The bias reaches δ̂ too, through the omitted spatial structure.

Covariate-distance W

When locations are unknown, Fülle & Otto (2024) build W from distances between units' covariates, following Santi et al. (2021). covariate_weights builds that matrix:

  • it standardises each covariate;
  • it then takes k nearest neighbours with equal weights.
W = covariate_weights(hcat(size_rank, sector_beta, leverage), 10)
m = fit_spatial_log_garch(returns, W)

A proxy W is a misspecified W. None of the three papers gives a way to measure from the data how close it is to the true one. Santi et al.'s measure, cor(W_X u, W u), needs the true W. The Monte Carlo's design C shows what a poor proxy does to λ̂ and ρ̂. Read ρ̂ from a proxy W as evidence that the variance has spatial structure, not as an estimate of how much.

Fülle & Otto also choose the covariates by forward selection on the likelihood. That search is not in the package: nothing corrects the inference that follows it.
