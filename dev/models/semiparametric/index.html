Semiparametric Models

Every other spatial model in this package fixes the functional form of the covariates. This page covers three estimators that leave part of it unknown, and the diagnostic that has to come with them. They implement roadmap #13, first pass.

FunctionModelSource
fit_partially_linear_sary = ρWy + Xβ + m(z) + ε, m unknownSu & Jin (2010)
closed_form_rhothe same, ρ without a log-determinantSmirnov (2022); Geniaux (2026)
spectral_overlaphow much of W's dominant modes a smoother reproducesGeniaux (2026)
fit_tv_coef_sarY_t = ρWY_t + X_tβ(t/T) + α + e_t, β(·) unknownLiang, Gao & Gong (2022)

The partially linear SAR

For a fixed ρ, Su & Jin estimate m by a local-polynomial regression of (I − ρW)y − Xβ on z. The smoother matrix S depends on z and the bandwidth only, never on ρ. That is the property everything here rests on: after residualising on I − S, the concentrated likelihood in ρ has exactly the shape fit_spatial maximises, with the log-determinant kept.

m = fit_partially_linear_sar(y, X, z, W)                    # rule-of-thumb h
m = fit_partially_linear_sar(y, X, z, W; bandwidth = :lscv) # two-step LSCV
m.rho, m.beta, m.m_hat

Two practical rules:

  • No intercept in X. A local-polynomial smoother reproduces constants, so m absorbs the intercept; so does any column that is a function of z alone. The fit refuses such a design as rank deficient rather than reporting an unidentified coefficient.
  • Homoskedastic errors. Su & Jin's Assumption 2 rules out heteroskedasticity, under which the QMLE is generally inconsistent. The standard errors also assume normal errors: the extra term their sandwich carries for skewness and excess kurtosis is not included. The Monte Carlo below measures what that costs.

A large bandwidth turns the local-linear smoother into the projection on [1, z], and the fit then reproduces fit_spatial with z as a regressor: ρ̂, β̂, both standard errors and the log-likelihood agree to about 1e-11 (testset 41b).

The closed-form ρ

Smirnov's estimator replaces the likelihood's log-determinant by a quadratic quasi-score in the residuals of y, Wy and W²y. Here those residuals come from the partially linear model's own first stage, so the two estimators share it:

c = closed_form_rho(m)
c.rho, c.se_classical, c.se_hc0, c.se_shac

With a smoother in the first stage no consistency proof covers this estimator. Smirnov gives none at all; Geniaux proves consistency for a fixed spline basis, and a kernel smoother with a shrinking bandwidth is outside that proof. Its coverage is measured below rather than assumed.

The identification diagnostic

If z carries geography — coordinates, or a distance to a fixed point — the smoother can reproduce the same low-frequency spatial patterns that ρWy explains. The two then compete for them, and ρ loses its identification. Geniaux (2026) calls this the geoadditive trap. His diagnostic is the share of each of W's dominant spatial modes that the smoother can reproduce, π_k = ‖Sφ_k‖, summarised by its mean π̄. Every fit computes it:

m.overlap            # SpectralOverlapResult
m.overlap.pibar

The modes are the eigenvectors of the doubly-centred symmetrised W. Geniaux states the uncentred one; the centring is deliberate, because every smoother here reproduces constants and the top mode of an uncentred row-standardised W is nearly constant, so it would read close to 1 for any fit.

A measure, not a test — and not a causal statement

π̄ has no threshold and no null distribution in its source, so the package attaches no p-value and no verdict to it. A small π̄ says only that the smoother does not compete with ρWy for W's dominant modes, given that W is the true interaction structure. That is statistical identification of ρ, not causal identification of a spillover. The endogenous and correlated effects stay confounded (Manski's reflection problem), and in a cross-section spatial heterogeneity and spillovers are observationally equivalent (Gibbons & Overman 2012; Debarsy & Le Gallo 2024). Nothing here makes ρ̂, or the impacts computed from it, causal.

Time-varying coefficients

Liang, Gao & Gong's model lets the regressor coefficients drift as smooth functions of rescaled time τ = t/T, with individual fixed effects and a constant ρ. The estimator has the same structure as Su & Jin's: a local-linear smoother in time that does not depend on ρ, the fixed effects partialled out, and a concentrated likelihood that keeps T log|I − ρW|.

tv = fit_tv_coef_sar(y, hcat(ones(N * T), x), W, N, T)   # bandwidth by CV
tv.rho, tv.beta, tv.beta_se, tv.tau

The data are stacked N units within each of T periods, as in the package's other panels. The default bandwidth is the paper's leave-one-unit-out cross-validation. beta_se is pointwise and excludes the smoothing bias, of order h²β''(τ). A time-varying ρ is a different model, with no published method, and is not offered.

The band under-covers in the interior, not at the ends. That is the measured result (validation/mc_tv_coef.jl, 500 replications per cell, N ∈ {50, 100}, T ∈ {25, 50}, a gently and a strongly curved β):

bandwidthinterior coverage (τ ∈ [0.2, 0.8])at the endsfits refused
cross-validated h0.81–0.87 (minimum 0.77)0.92–0.96none
h/2, refitted0.92–0.94 (minimum 0.90)0.93–0.96up to 44 of 500 at T = 25

Cross-validation picks the bandwidth that minimises mean squared error, and that makes the squared bias comparable to the variance. That under-covers wherever β curves. At the ends the variance is larger, so the same bias costs less coverage. The varying bandwidth adds to it: beta_se is conditional on the h that was chosen, and h itself varies from sample to sample. With h held fixed and ρ, α known, beta_se matched the Monte Carlo spread to within 5%. With h chosen by CV it fell to 0.87–0.92 of it.

For a band to read as a confidence band, refit at a smaller bandwidth:

tv  = fit_tv_coef_sar(y, X, W, N, T)
tvu = fit_tv_coef_sar(y, X, W, N, T; bandwidth = tv.bandwidth / 2)

That is undersmoothing. It trades a wider band for one that is centred closer to β(τ). With short panels it can fail: at T = 25 the halved window was too thin on up to 9% of samples, and coverage of ρ fell to 0.91–0.93 for the strongly curved β.

A very large bandwidth turns the fit into a within regression on x, τ and τx, and ρ̂ then matches fit_spatial_panel(…; approach = DirectQML(bias_correct = false)) to about 1e-11 (testset 41f). The standard errors differ by a single convention: the paper's information scales the trace terms by T, the package's panel code by T − 1 under individual fixed effects.

What the Monte Carlo shows

Each cell uses 500 replications. W is a row-standardised 6-nearest-neighbour graph on uniform points, with true β = 1 and the rule-of-thumb bandwidth. In the safe design, z is drawn independently of location (Su & Jin's §6 design). In the trap, z is the coordinates, m is a smooth surface in them, and x carries the first coordinate. Skewed errors are a centred χ²₃/√6: skewness 1.6, excess kurtosis 4. Coverage is that of the nominal 95% interval. The closed-form columns are its three standard errors. The full table, with RMSE and the fits that needed a widened window, is in docs/AUDIT.md.

What it shows:

  • In the safe design the profile QML is sound. Coverage of ρ and β is between 0.944 and 0.968 in every cell, including the skewed ones. At these sample sizes, omitting Su & Jin's Ω costs nothing measurable.
  • The closed-form ρ has no reliable standard error. The classical one is conservative, and at ρ = 0.75 it covers at 0.996–1.000. HC0 under-covers everywhere, at 0.896–0.930. SHAC reaches nominal only for normal errors at n = 1000, and stays at 0.926–0.936 under skewed errors. This matches what Geniaux reports for his spline version. With a kernel first stage it is a measurement, not a theorem. Use the QML interval for inference, and the closed form as a check.
  • The trap is real, and more data does not get you out of it. ρ̂ is biased down by 0.035–0.099. The bias halves from n = 400 to n = 1000, but the standard error shrinks too, so the QML coverage of ρ stays between 0.43 and 0.69. Coverage of β does not suffer. No standard error repairs this. The bias is the problem, not the variance.
  • π̄ separated the two designs. Its mean was 0.08–0.12 in the safe design and falling with n, against 0.58–0.60 in the trap and flat in n. That is a comparison of two designs, not a calibrated threshold: π̄ depends on n, W and the bandwidth, and only its mean was recorded. It does not license a cut-off for other data.
