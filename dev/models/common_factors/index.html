Common Factors and Spatio-Temporal Models

Every panel estimator elsewhere in this package assumes cross-sectional correlation is weak: that W governs it, so conditioning on the spatial lag is enough. The panel literature separates that from strong dependence driven by unobserved common factors λᵢ'fₜ, which no weight matrix absorbs.

This page covers the pair of things that case needs — tests that say which kind of dependence is present, and estimators that survive the strong kind — together with the space-time autoregression and the impulse responses that go with it.

Runnable: examples/27_common_factors.jl for the diagnostics and the loading-distribution table, examples/28_spatio_temporal.jl for STAR, impulse responses, forecasting and the rotation indeterminacy.

The one table to read first

Every design below has a genuine common factor. All that changes is the distribution of the loadings. Measured bias in ρ̂ at a true ρ = 0.4, N = 42, T = 30, 200 replications:

mean 0, random−0.004+0.003 / 0.068−0.008 / 0.059−0.002 / 0.025
mean 0, balanced ±1+0.329+0.373 ⚠+0.005 / 0.100−0.023 / 0.035

(bias / RMSE where both are shown.)

Nothing about this is the expected "more robust is safer" story:

  • A time effect is a factor with equal loadings. TimeFE subtracts the cross-sectional mean at each period, so a factor with λᵢ ≡ λ is removed to machine precision — measured at 5.55e-16. What a time effect can reach is the mean of the loadings, and nothing else.
  • When the loadings have a strong common mean, TwoWayFE wins outright (RMSE 0.029 against CCE's 0.047). It is the correctly specified parsimonious model, and CCE spends N·q projected columns for nothing. Reaching for the more general estimator costs you here.
  • When the loadings are balanced in sign, TwoWayFE is worse than doing nothing — bias +0.373 against NoFE's +0.329. Removing a cross-sectional mean that is already zero destroys variation and leaves the factor untouched. This is the case CCE and interactive FE exist for.

So the loading structure decides the estimator, and the next section is about finding out what it is.

Diagnosing it: and why one statistic is not enough

pesaran_cd implements the CD statistic of Pesaran (2015). Its implicit null is weak dependence, not independence — his eq. (12), H₀w : α < (2−ε)/4 with T = O(Nᵋ). The implementation reproduces his own headline result exactly (N = 100, T = 50, 3,000 reps, 5% level):

true α0.000.100.250.400.500.75
rejection0.0480.0540.0550.1440.5491.000

Correctly sized across [0, ¼], over-rejecting above — the transition sits precisely at the boundary he states.

alpha_exponent estimates the exponent itself, following Bailey, Kapetanios & Pesaran (2016), and enforces their two thresholds: α is not identified at or below 1/2, where the function throws rather than returning a number for a parameter that does not exist, and valid inference needs α > 4/7.

The configuration where both of them fail

With loadings that cancel — the ±1 row of the table above — ρ̂ᵢⱼ ∝ λᵢλⱼ does not accumulate, and:

loadingsCD rejectionα identified?mean |ρ̂|
independent0.047no (correctly)0.104
homogeneous1.000yes, 300/3000.496
mean-zero random0.348sometimes0.293
balanced ±10.911, all negativeno, 0/3000.911

Two distinct failures, and neither is a quiet non-rejection. With random mean-zero loadings CD's power collapses to about a third and does not recover as N grows (0.377 at N = 10, 0.343 at N = 160) — it is structural. With balanced ±1 loadings CD rejects 91% of the time but the statistic is negative, reporting a strong common factor as negative average dependence. And alpha_exponent refuses outright, because the cross-sectional average cancels and Var(x̄ₜ) collapses.

What survives is the average absolute correlation, reported as abs_rho_bar. Against its independence benchmark sqrt(2/(πT)) — measured 0.1036 against a predicted 0.1030 — it flags 1.000 of mean-zero-loading draws where the signed statistic manages 0.348.

dependence_verdict reads all of this together, and is the recommended entry point:

m = fit_spatial_panel(SAR(), NoFE(), W, X, y, N, T)
v = dependence_verdict(residuals(m), N, T)
v.kind    # :weak, :semi_weak, :strong or :unidentified
println(v.recommendation)

CD versus the LM statistic it replaces

variant = :lm gives the Breusch–Pagan statistic in Pesaran's scaled form. It is offered to reproduce the comparison, not as a recommendation. On estimated residuals — which is the case his critique is about, since E(Tρ̂²−1) is mis-centred when the residuals are fitted — it fails badly, and worsens monotonically with N exactly as he says:

Estimating it

fit_sp_cce — common correlated effects

Projects each unit's series off the cross-sectional averages [ȳₜ, X̄ₜ], unit by unit, which is what permits heterogeneous loadings and distinguishes CCE from a time effect. The residualised system is then estimated by 2SLS with instruments [X̃, WX̃, W²X̃].

A likelihood is deliberately not used: projecting off the factor proxies makes the error covariance non-scalar, so a QML written for spherical errors would be misspecified, and the log-determinant it would buy is not worth a wrong variance.

fit_interactive_fe — Bai (2009) iterative principal components

Profiles ρ out on a one-dimensional search, and at each trial ρ alternates between least squares with the factor space projected off, and re-extracting the factors by eigendecomposition of the residual panel.

Factors and loadings are identified only up to rotation. For any invertible R, (FR, ΛR⁻ᵀ) fits identically, so comparing m.factors across fits is meaningless. What is identified is the space they span:

factor_projection(m)    # F(F'F)⁻¹F' -- compare THIS

Measured, two fits from different starts agree on the projection to 5.2e-10 and on ρ̂/β̂ to ~1e-9, while the raw factors differ. A sign flip — the simplest rotation — leaves the projection unchanged to exactly 0.0e0.

n_factors = 0 removes the factor step entirely, and the estimator then reproduces fit_spatial_panel with the log-likelihood agreeing to 0.000e+00.

Space-time dynamics

fit_star fits Pfeifer & Deutsch's (1980) space-time autoregression,

yₜ = Σₖ Σₗ φₖₗ W⁽ᵏ⁾ yₜ₋ₗ + Xₜβ + εₜ

Every spatial lag is also temporally lagged, so all regressors are predetermined and least squares is consistent — no Jacobian, and arbitrary lag orders in both dimensions are affordable. The price is explicit: there is no contemporaneous Wyₜ, so within-period spatial feedback is ruled out. That specification is fit_spatial_panel, and neither nests the other.

Stationarity is the spectral radius of the L·N companion matrix, not a condition on any single Aₗ, and it is computed rather than assumed. For a row-normalised W the leading eigenvalue is exactly 1, so for A₁ = aI + bW the familiar |a| + |b| < 1 is not merely sufficient but exactly the boundary; drop the normalisation and the same coefficients diverge at |a| + |b| = 0.8, because the spectrum then reaches 3.65 on the same lattice. With L > 1 there is no coefficient sum to check at all.

That companion form is the same statement as the paper's own stationarity condition, which is determinantal: every x solving det[xᴸ I − Σₗ Aₗ xᴸ⁻ˡ] = 0 lies inside the unit circle. Evaluating that determinant at the companion eigenvalues gives a maximum modulus of 6.6e-27, so the two are not merely compatible — they are checked against each other.

W⁽ᵏ⁾: powers of W, or order-k neighbours?

spatial_weights chooses what W⁽ᵏ⁾ means:

valueW⁽ᵏ⁾
:powers (default)Wᵏmatrix powers
:ordersorder_weights(W, k)Pfeifer & Deutsch's definition

The paper defines a spatial lag operator whose weights are non-zero only between kth-order neighbours, each matrix row-standardised in its own right. Wᵏ is a different object: it counts walks, is non-zero on its own diagonal, and double-counts paths. The default is :powers because that is what this function has always computed and what its tests pin — a citation read more carefully is not a reason to change the arithmetic under a user who has shipped results.

The two coincide at k = 0 and k = 1, so at the default spatial_lags = 1 the choice is vacuous: coefficients, log-likelihood and spectral radius agree to 0.000e+00, and the test asserts == rather than a tolerance.

They diverge from k = 2. On a 5×4 rook lattice the order-2 matrix has 92 non-zeros with none on the diagonal, while W² has 112 with 20 on the diagonal. A lattice has no triangles, so every length-2 walk between distinct sites joins a distance-2 pair and the off-diagonal supports agree exactly (112 − 20 = 92). The whole difference is that W² puts weight on the unit itself and offers it as a second-order spatial lag. Fitting the same data both ways, the coefficient on that term is −0.1069 under :powers against −0.0528 under :orders — it roughly halves, because under :powers it is partly picking up an own-lag effect that y(t-1) is already in the design to carry. Coefficient names record which you got: W^2 y(t-1) against W⁽²⁾ y(t-1).

The two conventions also fail differently, and each reports its own diagnosis. Under :orders, S past the graph's diameter makes some W⁽ᵏ⁾ empty and the refusal names the diameter. Under :powers the same S is refused for rank deficiency instead, because Wᵏ becomes dependent on lower powers.

space_time_irf traces a shock through both dimensions, Ψ₀ = I, Ψₕ = Σₗ Aₗ Ψₕ₋ₗ with Aₗ = Σₖ φₖₗ W⁽ᵏ⁾, splitting each horizon into an own effect and a spillover. Summed over horizons it converges to the long-run multiplier (I − ΣₗAₗ)⁻¹, which is the identity it is tested against — agreement to 1.1e-15.

`cumulative` accumulates the summaries, not the matrices

psi[h+1] is always ∂yₜ₊ₕ/∂εₜ', the response at horizon h, whatever cumulative is set to. The keyword controls own, spillover and total. To compare against (I − ΣₗAₗ)⁻¹ directly, sum psi yourself — or read total[end], which is that sum's mean row sum.

The path is what the IRF adds over the multiplier, and the timing is not incidental: on a 7×6 lattice with φ̂ = (0.393, 0.396) the own effect peaks at h = 0 while the spillover peaks at h = 2, and the spillover/own ratio rises monotonically from there. A single direct/indirect/total decomposition is the h → ∞ column of that path, so for any question with a policy horizon shorter than convergence it is the wrong summary.

spatial_forecast iterates the same recursion forward, dynamically: forecasts are substituted for realisations once the data runs out. The model does not retain y, so the last L periods must be passed as y_history — omitting them throws rather than defaulting to zero.
