Spatial Causal Inference

Overview

Difference-in-differences assumes that a unit's outcome responds to its own treatment and nothing else — SUTVA. Geographic spillovers violate that by construction, which is the premise of this whole package. When a policy reaches a unit's neighbours, two things go wrong at once in a conventional DiD: the spillover is pushed into the error term, where it is correlated with treatment by construction, and the control group is no longer untreated. Both biases land on the estimated treatment effect.

The estimator here puts the spillover in the regression instead, where it can be measured.

\[y_{it} = \mu_i + \gamma_t + \tau \, D_i \text{post}_t + \delta \, E_i \text{post}_t + \varepsilon_{it}\]

with $E = WD$ the exposure: the fraction of $i$'s neighbours that are treated. For two periods, first-differencing removes $\mu_i$ exactly and leaves an ordinary least squares problem, so $\tau$ and $\delta$ are recovered without approximation.

Exposure mapping

An exposure mapping is what makes a spillover estimable at all: it replaces "treatment might spread somehow" with a named, finite summary of the treatment around each unit.

em = exposure(D, W)                  # fraction of neighbours treated
em = exposure(D, W; order = 2)       # first and second rings, separately
em = exposure(D, W; standardize = false)   # a count rather than a fraction

Rings read a spillover at a distance where mechanical displacement is implausible, which is often the point of the exercise:

near = ring_exposure(D, coords, 0.0, 10.0)
far  = ring_exposure(D, coords, 10.0, 25.0)

Every spillover estimate is relative to the mapping that produced it. A $\delta$ estimated on five nearest neighbours answers a different question from one estimated on a 50 km band, and neither is "the" spillover — which is why ExposureMap is carried in the result rather than discarded after the fit.

Check what the design can identify, before fitting

$\delta$ is identified by controls that differ in exposure. If every control has a treated neighbour there is no unexposed baseline and $\delta$ is absorbed by the constant; if no control has one, $\delta$ cannot be separated from the direct effect at all. This is a property of the design and the weight matrix alone, so it needs no outcome and should be checked first:

rep = check_interference(em)
rep.identified || @warn rep.message
rep.n_control_unexposed      # the units carrying the identification

A design that is outright rank deficient is refused by name when you try to fit it, rather than returning a number that cannot be interpreted.

Fitting

m = fit_spatial_did(y, D, W)                 # y is N x 2
m.att_direct          # effect on a treated unit of its own treatment
m.att_spill_control   # the policy's reach beyond its target

Four effects are reported, not three, because the spillover onto treated units and the spillover onto controls are evaluated at different exposure means and are not interchangeable:

FieldMeaning
att_directeffect on a treated unit of its own treatment
att_spill_treatedeffect on a treated unit of its neighbours' treatment
att_spill_controleffect on a control unit of its neighbours' treatment
att_total_treatedatt_direct + att_spill_treated

att_spill_treated is part of what a naive DiD would report as the direct effect. Each quantity is a fixed linear combination of the coefficient vector, so its standard error is exact rather than approximated.

Staggered rollout

Pass the full treatment path as an $N \times T$ matrix and the estimator switches to a two-way within transform:

m = fit_spatial_did(y, D_path, W)     # D_path[i, t] = 1 once i is treated

With homogeneous effects this is as exact as the two-period path. With heterogeneous effects it is not, and the error is large: measured on noiseless three-cohort rollouts it runs from 17% to 67%, and its sign is not predictable. Spatially contiguous adoption cohorts — which is how rollouts usually happen — are the worst case, because cohort identity is then nearly collinear with the exposure column. The fit_spatial_did docstring has the measured table. For a staggered design with suspected heterogeneity, estimate cohorts separately and aggregate deliberately.

Standard errors

The default is cluster-robust on unit. That is a measured choice rather than a conventional one, and it came out opposite to what was expected:

EstimatorCoverage of a nominal 95% interval
:cluster (default)95.0%
:ols96.5%
:conley, cutoff 1.594.5%
:conley, cutoff 6.085.3%

Conley's spatial HAC degrades as the cutoff widens, and it degrades identically when there is no spatial correlation at all — so the decay is the finite-sample cost of estimating more pair covariances, not the correction doing its job.

The reason the spatial correction matters less here than one would expect is the exposure column itself. Including $WD$ means $\tau$ is identified off the contrast between a unit and its neighbours, and that contrast differences out the component of the error they share — which is exactly the spatially correlated part.

A spatial HAC remains the right instrument for a spatially correlated regressor, and :conley is available for designs where the exposure column does not absorb the dependence:

m = fit_spatial_did(y, D, W; vcov = :conley, coords = coords, dist_cutoff = 2.5)

If you use it, report more than one cutoff and prefer the narrow end. Full figures, including the nine configurations the ordering was checked across, are in the fit_spatial_did docstring.

Synthetic control

Difference-in-differences needs a control group and a common pre-trend. The comparative case study has neither: one treated region, a long pre-period, and a few dozen untreated regions that are not interchangeable with it. Synthetic control builds the comparison instead of assuming it — a weighted average of untreated units chosen to track the treated unit before the intervention, used as its counterfactual after.

scm = fit_synthetic_control(Y, 41, 18; W = W, coords = coords)
scm.att                                    # mean post-period gap
pt  = placebo_test(scm, Y; W = W, coords = coords)
sg  = spillover_gaps(scm, Y; W = W, coords = coords)

For treated unit i, the weights solve

minimise   (x_i - X0 w)' (x_i - X0 w) + λ Σ_j d_ij w_j
subject to w ≥ 0,  Σ_j w_j = 1

a convex program over the unit simplex. It is solved by accelerated projected gradient with the exact Lipschitz step, and the returned point carries its own KKT certificate (kkt_gap, kkt_violation) rather than an assurance — unconverged weights are otherwise indistinguishable from converged ones.

The spatial problem: a contaminated donor pool

If the intervention spills over, the treated unit's neighbours are not untreated. They are also the units a synthetic control is most likely to select, because they resemble the treated unit most. The spillover then sits inside the comparison and the estimate is pulled toward zero.

This is not a statistical tendency to be hoped against — it is an identity. The spillover falls in the post-treatment periods only, so the weights, fitted on pre-treatment data, are untouched by it. The bias is therefore exactly the weight placed on contaminated donors times their exposure times the spillover:

att(contaminated pool) = att(clean pool) − Σ_{j ∈ neighbours} w_j · E_j · spillover

Testset 35f pins that identity to 1e-12 at four spillover sizes.

donor_pool = :exclude_neighbours, the default, drops every untreated unit adjacent to a treated one under W — reusing exposure to find them — and records which units went. donor_pool = :all is the conventional choice and is wrong whenever the spillover is real.

The trap is that the contaminated fit looks better. In examples/24_synthetic_control.jl the all-donor fit puts 90.1% of its weight on regions the policy had reached, achieves a pre-period RMSPE of 0.045 against the screened fit's 0.097, and is wrong by −0.433 against a true effect of 3.0 — where the screened fit is wrong by 0.005. A good pre-period fit is a necessary check, never a sufficient one, and it cannot detect this problem at all, because the contamination is invisible before treatment.

The penalty

Unpenalised weights are frequently not unique. With more donors than pre-periods — the usual case — several weight vectors fit the pre-period identically and imply different counterfactuals, and which one is returned is an accident of the algorithm. Abadie & L'Hour (2021) break the tie with a penalty on pairwise discrepancy between the treated unit and each donor it draws on; penalty_metric = :spatial makes that discrepancy geographic.

What the penalty buys is proximity, not sparsity, and it is paid for in pre-period fit. Measured on a 9×9 lattice with spatially unstructured characteristics:

λdonorsmean donor distancepre-RMSPE
0.0033.1670.076
0.1043.1390.076

Mean distance falls monotonically; the number of donors does not. On the same lattice with spatially smooth characteristics the penalty changes nothing at all — the unpenalised fit had already chosen the two nearest donors, because there proximity and fit agree. Worth knowing in both directions: the penalty is redundant when characteristics are smooth, and it is doing real work when they are not.

As λ → ∞ the support collapses into the set of minimum-distance donors (a single donor when distances are distinct, the tied set otherwise).

Predictor weighting

Classic synthetic control nests two optimisations: inner weights w given a predictor-weighting matrix V, and an outer search over V. This implementation takes V = I on standardised predictors and does not search, deliberately. The outer problem is documented as non-unique, so two equally good V give different weights and different counterfactuals — the estimate stops being reproducible, which is not a trade this package makes. standardize = true scales each predictor to unit variance, which is the part of V's job that is well posed.

Inference

There are no standard errors, and SyntheticControlResult deliberately does not define vcov or stderror. The weights are a constrained optimum rather than an estimator with a sampling distribution of known form, and the gap is a difference between an observed path and a constructed one.

placebo_test reassigns the treatment to units that were never treated, refits, and ranks the observed post/pre RMSPE ratio against the placebo distribution. Dividing by the pre-period fit is what makes placebo ratios comparable: a donor the pool tracks badly before treatment shows a large post-period gap for reasons unrelated to any policy. All binomial(J, m) assignments are enumerated when that number is small enough, giving an exact permutation test; otherwise they are sampled from a seeded RNG, and the result records which happened.

A p-value is a test statistic, so its null distribution is measured under the standing rule in docs/AUDIT.md. No treatment effect anywhere, 7×7 lattice, 44 clean donors, 1,000 replications:

ρp ≤ 0.022p ≤ 0.05p ≤ 0.10mean p
0.00.0230.0460.0950.503
0.60.0170.0450.0940.492
0.90.0410.0680.1250.438

Correctly sized with no spatial correlation and with moderate correlation — a mean p-value of 0.50 is what exchangeability predicts. At ρ = 0.9 it over-rejects by roughly a third of the nominal level, about 2.6 standard errors at this replication count. The planning expectation was that spatial dependence would break the test badly; it does not, because the ratio statistic scales the gap by the pre-period fit and spatial dependence largely divides out.

The hard limit is granularity, not size. The smallest attainable p-value is 1/(1+R), so a single treated unit with 18 donors cannot return anything below 0.053 — it cannot reject at the 5% level however large the effect. min_attainable_p reports the bound.

Spillovers as an estimand

The units screened out of the donor pool were screened out because the intervention reached them, which is itself worth measuring. spillover_gaps synthesises each excluded neighbour from the same clean pool and reads its gap — the synthetic control counterpart of att_spill_control in fit_spatial_did.

Read rmspe_pre per unit before any of those gaps. In testset 35f two of four neighbours are exactly reproducible from the clean pool and return the spillover to 1e-10; the other two cannot be fitted at all (pre-period RMSPE larger by more than ten orders of magnitude) and their gaps are not spillovers. The unweighted average over all four is consequently off, and the fix is reading the diagnostic rather than the function silently discarding units.

Shift-share designs

A shift-share, or Bartik, regressor is built from common shocks and region-specific exposure:

\[X_i = \sum_{k} w_{ik} \, g_k\]

with $w_{ik}$ region $i$'s share of industry $k$ and $g_k$ the industry's national shock. It is used directly, or as an instrument for an endogenous regional variable — the canonical Bartik design.

This belongs in a spatial package for a reason that is almost the opposite of the rest of it. Everywhere else here, dependence travels through geography and the fix is a spatial weight matrix. Here it travels through shared industry mix: two regions with the same industries have mechanically correlated values of $X$, and therefore correlated residuals, no matter how far apart they are. Two counties on opposite coasts with identical industrial structure are as dependent as neighbours, and a spatial HAC correction cannot see it.

Build the design, then ask how big it really is

design = shift_share_design(W, g)          # W is N x K, g is length K
conc   = exposure_concentration(design)
conc.effective_shocks                       # compare with conc.n_shocks

Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel's identification argument requires a Herfindahl condition on average exposure, $\sum_k \hat{s}_k^2 \to 0$: no finite set of shocks may drive the estimate. effective_shocks is the reciprocal of that sum, reported because a count is easier to read.

Read it first. The asymptotics in this design are in the number of shocks, not the number of regions, and every inference below degrades together as the effective count falls. A study with 300 regions and 400 industries whose effective count is 12 is a twelve-observation study.

The design has $K$ observations, not $N$

agg = shock_aggregate(design, y)
agg.beta_equiv == fit_shift_share(y, design).beta   # to machine precision

Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel (2022, eq. 4) show the shift-share estimate is numerically identical to an exposure-weighted estimate computed from K shock-level observations:

\[\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_k \hat{s}_k g_k \bar{y}_k}{\sum_k \hat{s}_k g_k \bar{x}_k}\]

This is an identity, not an approximation, and it is the clearest statement of where the information in the design actually is.

Standard errors

fit = fit_shift_share(y, design)                    # vcov = :akm by default
fit = fit_shift_share(y, design; treatment = t)     # the IV design

Adão, Kolesár & Morales (2019) show by placebo that conventional standard errors in this design reject a true null in up to 55% of samples at the 5% level. Their estimator (eq. 29) is

\[\mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_k \mathring{X}_k^2 \hat{R}_k^2}}{\sum_i \tilde{X}_i^2}, \qquad \hat{R}_k = \sum_i w_{ik}\,\hat{\varepsilon}_i\]

where $\tilde{X}$ is the regressor with controls partialled out and $\mathring{X}$ is the vector of coefficients from regressing $\tilde{X}$ on the share matrix. $\mathring{X}$ is not the shock vector $g$; the two coincide only with no controls and a square invertible share matrix.

Coverage of a nominal 95% interval, measured over 2,000 replications with N = 300, shares and residuals fixed and the shocks resampled — the framework the estimator is derived in. "Sector frac" is the fraction of residual variance carried by an industry-level component:

Three things worth taking from it.

The failure is real and large. HC1 rejects a true null in up to 71% of samples on these designs — worse than the paper's headline figure, on a design built to be realistic rather than adversarial.

Clustering on each region's dominant industry does not fix it. That is the intuitive repair, and the cluster column tracks HC1 almost exactly, because the dependence runs through the entire share vector rather than through any one industry. This is worth stating plainly because the cluster-robust machinery is available elsewhere in this package and would be the natural thing to reach for.

The AKM standard error itself degrades when shocks are few, to 0.864 at 20 effective shocks and 0.827 at 38 with concentrated exposure. Adão, Kolesár & Morales anticipate this: eq. 29 generalises a cluster-robust formula and inherits its downward bias from estimation noise in the residuals, which is why they also propose a null-imposed interval (their Remark 6). That variant is not implemented here. Where exposure_concentration reports few effective shocks, treat the interval as optimistic.

Note also the first row: with no industry component at all, AKM costs a little — 0.930 against HC1's 0.950. That is the price of robustness when it is not needed, and it is small.

Exogeneity

bal = balance_test(design, hcat(pretrend, demographics))

The identifying assumption is quasi-random assignment of the shocks, not of the shares — shares are allowed to be endogenous. So the balance test belongs at shock level: form the exposure-weighted average of each residualised characteristic and regress it on the shock, weighting by average exposure (Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel 2022, §4.3). Conventional robust standard errors are valid there, which is the practical payoff of the equivalence above.

Measured size, 2,000 replications, with the characteristic itself carrying an industry component so that it is structured rather than iid:

The test is correctly sized only when the effective number of shocks is large. At 9 effective shocks it rejects a true null 23% of the time at a nominal 5%, and the joint test degrades faster still since it estimates an $M \times M$ covariance from as many observations as there are shocks.

The consequence is uncomfortable and worth saying: in a concentrated design, a passed balance test is close to uninformative. It is reported with a warning rather than left to be read as reassurance.

Which industries identify the estimate

rot = rotemberg_weights(y, treatment, design)
rot.order[1:5]        # the industries worth defending

Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin & Swift (2020, Prop. 3) decompose the estimate into one just-identified estimate per industry:

\[\hat{\beta} = \sum_k \hat{\alpha}_k \hat{\beta}_k, \qquad \sum_k \hat{\alpha}_k = 1\]

Both the unit sum and the decomposition hold exactly. Negative weights are expected — the paper says so explicitly, and under constant effects they pose no conceptual problem — so they are reported rather than suppressed.

The use is blunt: if five industries carry most of the weight mass, the study rests on those five, and they are the ones whose exogeneity a reader should interrogate.

Spatial autocorrelation, and why it is not the issue

shift_share_moran(design, W_spatial; fit = fit)

Industry shares cluster geographically, so Moran's I on them is almost always significant. That is normal and not a defect. What the diagnostic is for is documenting the spatial structure while making clear that a spatial correction is the wrong tool: use vcov = :akm for inference and this for description.
